# Topic 17: Rocket Science Fundamentals
## 🚀 THE Core "Rocket Revolution" Topic

### Learn This Topic

#### What Is a Rocket?
A rocket is a vehicle that carries everything it needs for propulsion — both fuel and oxidiser — unlike a jet engine that takes oxygen from the atmosphere. This makes rockets the only vehicles that can work in the vacuum of space.

#### How Rockets Work — Newton's Third Law
Rockets work on **Newton's Third Law of Motion**: "For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction."
- The rocket engine burns propellant (fuel + oxidiser) to produce hot, high-pressure gases
- These gases are expelled downward through a nozzle at very high speed (the **action**)
- The rocket is pushed upward in the opposite direction (the **reaction**)
- This is the same principle as a balloon flying around a room when you let go of it

#### Conservation of Momentum
- Total momentum of a system remains constant if no external forces act on it
- Rocket + exhaust gas system: as gas shoots backward with momentum, the rocket gains equal momentum forward
- The faster the gas is expelled, the more thrust the rocket produces

#### Tsiolkovsky and the Rocket Equation
- **Konstantin Tsiolkovsky** (1857–1935, Russia): "Father of Astronautics"
- Published the rocket equation in 1903 (same year as Wright Brothers' first flight!)
- Key insight: a rocket's final speed depends on (a) how fast it expels gas (exhaust velocity) and (b) the ratio of its initial mass (with fuel) to its final mass (without fuel)
- This is why rockets must carry enormous amounts of fuel — most of a rocket's mass at launch is propellant
- Example: Saturn V weighed 2,970 tonnes at launch but only ~130 tonnes reached orbit

#### Types of Rocket Propulsion

**1. Solid Propellant Rockets**
- Fuel and oxidiser are pre-mixed into a solid compound (like a giant firework)
- Once ignited, burns until all propellant is consumed — **cannot be shut down or throttled**
- Advantages: simple, reliable, can be stored for long periods, no leaks
- Disadvantages: cannot be controlled once lit, lower efficiency than liquid
- Examples: PSLV first stage (S139, one of world's largest solid rocket motors), SLV-3, SSLV (all solid stages), Space Shuttle SRBs, S200 boosters on LVM3

**2. Liquid Propellant Rockets**
- Fuel and oxidiser stored separately as liquids, pumped into combustion chamber
- **Can be throttled, shut down, and restarted**
- Advantages: higher efficiency, controllable, restartable
- Disadvantages: complex plumbing (turbopumps, valves, pipes), propellants may be toxic or corrosive
- Common propellant combinations:
  - UDMH + N₂O₄ (hypergolic — ignites on contact, used in Vikas engine)
  - RP-1 (kerosene) + LOX (used in Saturn V first stage, Falcon 9)
  - Liquid Hydrogen (LH₂) + Liquid Oxygen (LOX) — cryogenic (see below)
- Examples: PSLV second stage (Vikas engine), GSLV core stage

**3. Cryogenic Propulsion**
- Uses **Liquid Hydrogen (LH₂)** as fuel and **Liquid Oxygen (LOX)** as oxidiser
- Both must be stored at extremely low temperatures (LH₂ at -253°C, LOX at -183°C) — hence "cryogenic" (cryo = extremely cold)
- **Highest efficiency** (specific impulse) of chemical rockets — exhaust product is just water!
- Very difficult to develop — requires special materials, insulation, turbopumps that work at extreme cold
- **India's cryogenic engine story**: In 1991, India signed a deal with Russia's Glavkosmos for cryogenic engines and technology transfer. The USA pressured Russia under MTCR (Missile Technology Control Regime), and Russia cancelled the technology transfer (delivered only 7 ready-made engines, no know-how). ISRO then developed its own cryogenic engine — took nearly 20 years! First successful flight with indigenous cryogenic engine: **GSLV-D5 (January 5, 2014)** with CE-7.5 engine. Later developed the more powerful **CE-20** engine for LVM3.
- Examples: GSLV upper stage (CE-7.5), LVM3 upper stage (CE-20), Space Shuttle Main Engine, Ariane 5

**4. Semi-Cryogenic Engines**
- Use refined kerosene (RP-1) + LOX — only oxidiser is cryogenic
- Cheaper and easier to handle than full cryogenic, but less efficient
- ISRO is developing the SCE-200 semi-cryogenic engine for future rockets

**5. Ion/Electric Propulsion**
- Uses electric fields to accelerate ions (charged particles) to very high speeds
- Very low thrust (can't lift off from a planet) but extremely high efficiency
- Used for deep space missions and satellite station-keeping
- Examples: Dawn mission (NASA), Hayabusa (JAXA), some communication satellites
- Runs on solar power or nuclear power

**6. Hybrid Propulsion**
- Combines elements of solid and liquid — typically solid fuel + liquid oxidiser
- Can be throttled and shut down (unlike pure solid)
- Used by SpaceShipTwo (Virgin Galactic)

#### Key Concept: Specific Impulse (Isp)
- Measures how efficiently a rocket engine uses propellant
- Defined as: thrust produced per unit weight of propellant consumed per second
- Unit: seconds
- Higher Isp = more efficient engine
- Typical values:
  - Solid rockets: ~250 seconds
  - Liquid (UDMH/N₂O₄): ~290 seconds
  - Cryogenic (LH₂/LOX): ~450 seconds
  - Ion engines: ~3000+ seconds (but very low thrust)

#### Rocket Staging — Why Multi-Stage?
- A rocket carrying fuel for the entire journey would be enormously heavy
- **Solution: staging** — divide the rocket into stages, discard each empty stage as its fuel runs out
- Dropping empty tanks and engines reduces mass → remaining stages can accelerate more efficiently
- Most rockets use 2-4 stages:
  - Stage 1 (booster): largest, most thrust, lifts off the ground
  - Stage 2: accelerates in thinner atmosphere
  - Stage 3/upper stage: final push to orbit
- PSLV: 4 stages (alternating solid-liquid-solid-liquid)
- LVM3: 2 solid strap-on boosters (S200) + liquid core stage (L110) + cryogenic upper stage (C25)
- Saturn V: 3 stages (S-IC, S-II, S-IVB)

#### Payload and Payload Fairing
- **Payload**: the satellite or spacecraft the rocket carries to space
- **Payload fairing**: the protective nose cone that shields the payload during atmospheric flight
- Fairing is jettisoned once the rocket is above the dense atmosphere (~100-115 km)

#### Thrust and Thrust-to-Weight Ratio
- **Thrust**: force produced by the engine (measured in kilonewtons, kN, or meganewtons, MN)
- **Thrust-to-weight ratio**: must be >1 for the rocket to lift off (thrust must exceed weight)
- Saturn V: 34.5 MN thrust at liftoff (most powerful rocket flown until SLS/Starship)

#### India's Rocket Journey
1. **1963**: First sounding rocket (Nike-Apache) launched from Thumba — parts transported by bicycle and bullock cart!
2. **1967-70s**: Indigenous Rohini sounding rockets developed
3. **1979**: SLV-3 first attempt (failed)
4. **1980**: SLV-3 success — India becomes 6th nation with satellite launch capability
5. **1987-94**: ASLV (limited success, learning phase)
6. **1993-94**: PSLV development (D1 failed, D2 success)
7. **2001**: GSLV-D1 with Russian cryogenic upper stage
8. **2014**: GSLV-D5 with indigenous cryogenic engine — India masters cryogenic technology
9. **2017**: LVM3 first operational flight (GSLV MkIII-D1)
10. **2023**: SSLV becomes operational (SSLV-D2)
11. **Future**: Semi-cryogenic engines, RLV (Reusable Launch Vehicle), NGLV (Next Generation Launch Vehicle)

### Key Facts
- Newton's Third Law is the fundamental principle behind ALL rocket propulsion
- Tsiolkovsky published the rocket equation in 1903
- Robert Goddard launched the first liquid-fuelled rocket in 1926
- V-2 was the first rocket to reach space (1944)
- India's cryogenic engine was developed indigenously after technology denial
- PSLV alternates solid and liquid stages (S-L-S-L)
- Higher specific impulse = more efficient engine
- Cryogenic engines are the most efficient chemical rocket engines
- Staging allows rockets to shed dead weight during flight

### Important Terms
- **Propellant**: fuel + oxidiser together
- **Specific Impulse (Isp)**: efficiency measure of a rocket engine
- **Thrust**: force produced by the engine
- **Staging**: using multiple rocket sections that separate during flight
- **Payload**: the cargo (satellite/spacecraft) carried by the rocket
- **Payload Fairing**: protective nose cone
- **Cryogenic**: involving extremely low temperatures
- **Hypergolic**: propellants that ignite spontaneously on contact with each other
- **Nozzle**: the bell-shaped exit of a rocket engine that directs exhaust gases
- **Exhaust Velocity**: speed at which gases leave the rocket nozzle
- **Turbopump**: high-speed pump that feeds propellant into the combustion chamber
- **MTCR**: Missile Technology Control Regime (the agreement that blocked India's cryogenic tech transfer)

### Common Confusions
| Confusion | Clarification |
|-----------|---------------|
| Rockets push against air | NO — rockets work by Newton's 3rd Law (action-reaction), they work even better in vacuum (no air resistance) |
| Solid rockets can be turned off | NO — once ignited, solid rockets burn until propellant is exhausted |
| Cryogenic means super-hot | NO — cryogenic means extremely COLD (cryo = cold). Cryogenic fuels must be stored at very low temperatures |
| More stages = better | Not necessarily — each stage adds complexity and dead weight; 2-4 stages is optimal for most missions |
| India bought cryogenic technology from Russia | NO — Russia was pressured to cancel tech transfer. India developed its own cryogenic engine independently over ~20 years |
| Fuel and propellant are the same | Propellant = fuel + oxidiser. In space, rockets need both because there's no oxygen |

# Topic 18: Orbits and Orbital Mechanics

### Learn This Topic

#### What Is an Orbit?
An orbit is the curved path of an object around another object due to gravity. A satellite in orbit is actually **falling toward Earth continuously** but moving forward fast enough that it keeps "missing" the ground — the Earth's surface curves away at the same rate the satellite falls.

Isaac Newton's thought experiment: If you throw a ball horizontally from a very tall mountain, it falls to the ground. Throw it faster, it lands further away. Throw it fast enough (~28,000 km/h at LEO altitude), and it falls around the entire Earth — it's in orbit!

#### Orbital Velocity
- The speed needed to maintain an orbit at a given altitude
- Lower orbit → faster orbital velocity (closer to Earth, stronger gravity)
- LEO (~400 km): ~7.7 km/s (~27,700 km/h)
- GEO (35,786 km): ~3.07 km/s (~11,000 km/h)
- The Moon (~384,400 km): ~1.02 km/s

#### Types of Orbits

**1. Low Earth Orbit (LEO)**
- Altitude: 200–2,000 km
- Orbital period: ~90 minutes
- Used for: Earth observation, ISS (~400 km), Hubble (~547 km), many remote sensing satellites
- Advantages: close to Earth, less energy needed to reach, low communication delay
- Disadvantages: limited coverage area, atmospheric drag (needs periodic re-boost at very low altitudes)
- Indian satellites in LEO: IRS series, Cartosat, RISAT, AstroSat, XPoSat

**2. Medium Earth Orbit (MEO)**
- Altitude: 2,000–35,786 km
- Used for: navigation satellites
- GPS constellation: ~20,200 km altitude
- GLONASS: ~19,100 km
- NavIC (India): some satellites in MEO/inclined geosynchronous

**3. Geostationary Earth Orbit (GEO)**
- Altitude: exactly **35,786 km** above the equator
- Orbital period: exactly **24 hours** (matches Earth's rotation)
- Satellite appears **stationary** over one point on the equator
- Used for: communication satellites, weather satellites, TV broadcasting
- Advantage: ground antenna can point at fixed spot — no tracking needed
- Only possible over the equator (0° inclination)
- Indian satellites in GEO: INSAT series, GSAT series, CMS series
- "Clarke orbit" — named after Arthur C. Clarke who proposed it in 1945

**4. Geosynchronous Orbit (GSO)**
- Period = 24 hours (same as GEO) BUT can be inclined (not on equator)
- Traces a figure-8 pattern as seen from the ground
- NavIC uses some satellites in inclined GSO
- GEO is a special case of GSO (GSO with 0° inclination = GEO)

**5. Polar Orbit**
- Inclination: ~90° (passes over or near both poles)
- Provides complete coverage of Earth's surface over time
- As Earth rotates underneath, each orbit covers a different strip
- Used for: Earth observation, remote sensing, weather satellites
- Many IRS satellites use polar orbits

**6. Sun-Synchronous Orbit (SSO)**
- Special type of polar orbit
- Orbit plane maintains constant angle with respect to the Sun
- Satellite crosses the equator at the same local solar time every orbit
- Provides consistent lighting conditions — excellent for Earth imaging
- Typical altitude: 600–800 km
- Most of India's remote sensing satellites are in SSO
- PSLV specialises in launching satellites to SSO

**7. Highly Elliptical Orbit (HEO)**
- Very elongated (oval) orbit with a low perigee and high apogee
- Satellite spends most time near apogee (slows down at highest point — Kepler's 2nd Law)
- **Molniya orbit**: used by Russia for communication over high latitudes (period ~12 hours, high apogee over northern hemisphere)
- Mars Orbiter Mission used a highly elliptical Mars orbit

**8. Transfer Orbits**
- **Hohmann Transfer Orbit**: the most fuel-efficient way to move between two circular orbits
  - An elliptical orbit that touches both the starting and target orbits
  - Requires two burns: one to enter transfer orbit, one to circularise at destination
- **Geostationary Transfer Orbit (GTO)**: the elliptical orbit used to transfer satellites from LEO to GEO
  - Low point (perigee): ~200-300 km
  - High point (apogee): ~35,786 km
  - Satellite fires its own engine at apogee to circularise into GEO
  - GSLV and LVM3 place satellites into GTO; the satellite's own thruster does the rest

**9. Halo Orbits (Lagrange Points)**
- **Lagrange points**: five special positions in a two-body system (like Sun-Earth) where gravitational forces and orbital motion balance
- **L1**: between Sun and Earth (~1.5 million km from Earth toward Sun) — Aditya-L1 is here!
- **L2**: beyond Earth away from Sun (~1.5 million km) — JWST is here!
- **L3**: on opposite side of Sun from Earth
- **L4 and L5**: 60° ahead and behind Earth in its orbit (stable, "Trojan" points)
- Halo orbit: a satellite doesn't sit exactly at L1/L2 but orbits around the point in a "halo"
- Advantage of L1 for solar observation: unobstructed continuous view of Sun, no eclipses

#### Key Orbital Concepts

**Perigee and Apogee (for Earth orbits)**
- **Perigee**: closest point to Earth in an elliptical orbit
- **Apogee**: farthest point from Earth
- For orbits around the Sun: perihelion and aphelion
- For orbits around the Moon: perilune and apolune

**Inclination**
- Angle between the orbital plane and the equatorial plane
- 0° = equatorial orbit (GEO)
- 90° = polar orbit
- >90° = retrograde orbit

**Kepler's Laws (Quick Recap)**
1. Planets move in **elliptical** orbits with the Sun at one focus
2. A line from planet to Sun sweeps equal areas in equal times (moves faster when closer)
3. T² ∝ a³ (orbital period squared is proportional to semi-major axis cubed)

**Delta-v (Δv)**
- The total change in velocity a rocket must provide for a mission
- Measured in km/s or m/s
- More Δv needed = more fuel required
- Earth surface to LEO: ~9.4 km/s (including gravity and drag losses)
- LEO to GTO: ~2.5 km/s
- GTO to GEO: ~1.5 km/s
- This is why reaching GEO requires much bigger rockets than reaching LEO

#### Why Launch from Near the Equator?
- Earth rotates at ~1,670 km/h at the equator (465 m/s)
- Launching eastward from the equator gives a "free" velocity boost
- This reduces the fuel needed to reach orbit
- Closer to equator = bigger boost
- This is why Sriharikota (13.7°N) was chosen — it's the closest suitable coastal location to the equator in India
- French Guiana (ESA's launch site, ~5°N) is even closer to the equator

#### Orbital Decay and De-orbiting
- In LEO, trace amounts of atmosphere cause drag, slowly lowering the orbit
- Without re-boost, satellites eventually re-enter and burn up
- ISS needs periodic re-boosts to maintain orbit
- Guidelines: satellites should de-orbit within 25 years of end of life (to reduce space debris)

### Key Facts
- GEO altitude is exactly 35,786 km (orbital period = 24 hours)
- LEO is 200–2,000 km altitude, with ~90-minute orbital period
- Sun-synchronous orbits are ideal for Earth observation (consistent lighting)
- PSLV specialises in polar/sun-synchronous orbit launches
- Lagrange Point L1 (Sun-Earth) is ~1.5 million km from Earth — where Aditya-L1 operates
- Lagrange Point L2 is where JWST operates
- Arthur C. Clarke proposed the geostationary orbit concept in 1945
- Sriharikota is at ~13.7°N latitude — closer to equator = better for launches
- NavIC uses a mix of GEO and inclined GSO satellites

### Important Terms
- **LEO**: Low Earth Orbit (200–2,000 km)
- **MEO**: Medium Earth Orbit (2,000–35,786 km)
- **GEO**: Geostationary Earth Orbit (35,786 km, equatorial)
- **GSO**: Geosynchronous Orbit (24-hour period, any inclination)
- **SSO**: Sun-Synchronous Orbit
- **GTO**: Geostationary Transfer Orbit
- **Perigee/Apogee**: closest/farthest points in an Earth orbit
- **Inclination**: tilt of orbit relative to equator
- **Delta-v**: total velocity change needed for a manoeuvre
- **Hohmann Transfer**: fuel-efficient transfer between two orbits
- **Lagrange Points**: gravitational balance points in a two-body system (L1–L5)
- **Halo Orbit**: orbit around a Lagrange point
- **Orbital Period**: time for one complete orbit
- **Retrograde Orbit**: orbit opposite to the body's rotation

### Common Confusions
| Confusion | Clarification |
|-----------|---------------|
| GEO and GSO are the same | GEO is a subset of GSO. GEO is geosynchronous + equatorial (0° inclination). GSO can be inclined. |
| Satellites in GEO are not moving | They ARE moving (~3 km/s), but they match Earth's rotation so they APPEAR stationary from the ground |
| Higher orbit = faster satellite | OPPOSITE — higher orbit = slower velocity. LEO satellites travel ~7.7 km/s, GEO satellites ~3 km/s |
| All polar orbits are sun-synchronous | No — SSO is a specific type of polar orbit with a special precession rate |
| Lagrange points are fixed spots | Satellites don't sit still at L1/L2; they orbit around these points in halo or Lissajous orbits |
| Rockets always launch straight up | Rockets launch vertically but quickly turn eastward (gravity turn) to build horizontal velocity for orbit |
| Far side of Moon = dark side | The far side gets just as much sunlight as the near side. "Dark" means "unknown/unseen from Earth" |

## Topic 6 – The Moon

### Questions for Topic 6

### Question 1
What is the Moon?

A) A planet orbiting the Sun
B) Earth's only natural satellite
C) An asteroid captured by Earth
D) A dwarf planet

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** The Moon is Earth's only natural satellite — a celestial body that orbits our planet. It is the fifth-largest moon in the solar system and the largest relative to its host planet. Unlike dwarf planets (which orbit the Sun independently), the Moon is gravitationally bound to Earth.

**Difficulty:** Easy

**Source:** Planetary Science

### Question 2
What is the approximate diameter of the Moon?

A) ~1,737 km
B) ~3,474 km
C) ~6,371 km
D) ~12,742 km

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** The Moon's diameter is approximately 3,474 km, which is about one-quarter of Earth's diameter (12,742 km). This makes the Moon unusually large compared to its parent planet — no other planet in the solar system has a moon so large relative to itself (except the Pluto–Charon system).

**Difficulty:** Easy

**Source:** Planetary Science

### Question 3
What is the average distance from Earth to the Moon?

A) ~38,440 km
B) ~150,000 km
C) ~384,400 km
D) ~1,500,000 km

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** The average distance from Earth to the Moon is approximately 384,400 km. Light takes about 1.28 seconds to travel this distance. The actual distance varies because the Moon's orbit is elliptical — it ranges from about 356,500 km (perigee, closest) to 406,700 km (apogee, farthest).

**Difficulty:** Easy

**Source:** Planetary Science

### Question 4
What is the surface gravity of the Moon compared to Earth's?

A) About 1/2 of Earth's
B) About 1/4 of Earth's
C) About 1/6 of Earth's
D) About 1/10 of Earth's

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** The Moon's surface gravity is approximately 1/6 (about 16.6%) of Earth's. This means an astronaut who weighs 60 kg on Earth would feel as if they weigh only about 10 kg on the Moon. This low gravity is why Apollo astronauts could bound along the surface in great leaps despite heavy spacesuits.

**Difficulty:** Easy

**Source:** Planetary Science

### Question 5
Does the Moon have an atmosphere?

A) Yes, a thick nitrogen atmosphere like Earth
B) Yes, a thin carbon dioxide atmosphere like Mars
C) Virtually none — only a tenuous exosphere
D) Yes, but only on the far side

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** The Moon has virtually no atmosphere — only an extremely thin exosphere containing trace amounts of sodium, potassium, helium, and argon. Without a protective atmosphere, the Moon has no weather, no sound propagation, and no protection from meteorite impacts or solar radiation. This is also why temperature extremes are so severe (-173°C to 127°C).

**Difficulty:** Easy

**Source:** Planetary Science

### Question 6
Why does the same face of the Moon always point toward Earth?

A) The Moon does not rotate at all
B) The Moon is tidally locked — its rotation period equals its orbital period
C) Earth's atmosphere blocks our view of the other side
D) The far side is always dark so we cannot see it

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** The Moon is tidally locked to Earth, meaning its rotation period around its own axis (~27.3 days) is exactly the same as its orbital period around Earth (~27.3 days). As a result, the same hemisphere — the near side — always faces Earth. This synchronisation was caused by gravitational tidal forces between Earth and the Moon over billions of years.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** Planetary Science / Celestial Mechanics

### Question 7
What is the far side of the Moon?

A) The side of the Moon that never receives sunlight
B) The hemisphere of the Moon never visible from Earth, which receives equal sunlight as the near side
C) A dark region covered in permanent shadow
D) The side that always faces the Sun

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** The far side of the Moon is the hemisphere that never faces Earth due to tidal locking. Crucially, the far side is NOT a "dark side" — it receives just as much sunlight as the near side during the course of a lunar month. The term "dark side" is a common misconception. The far side was first photographed by the Soviet Luna 3 spacecraft in 1959.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** Planetary Science

### Question 8
Which spacecraft took the first photographs of the Moon's far side?

A) Apollo 11 (NASA, 1969)
B) Luna 3 (USSR, 1959)
C) Chandrayaan-1 (ISRO, 2008)
D) Ranger 7 (NASA, 1964)

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** The Soviet spacecraft Luna 3 took the first-ever photographs of the Moon's far side on October 7, 1959. These grainy but historic images revealed that the far side looks quite different from the near side — it has far fewer dark maria (lava plains) and is much more heavily cratered. Before Luna 3, no human had ever seen this hemisphere.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** Space Exploration History / USSR Lunar Programme

### Question 9
According to the leading scientific theory, how was the Moon formed?

A) It was captured from the asteroid belt by Earth's gravity
B) It formed at the same time as Earth from the solar nebula
C) A Mars-sized body called Theia collided with early Earth, and debris from the collision formed the Moon
D) It broke off from Earth due to rapid rotation

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** The Giant Impact Hypothesis is the leading theory for the Moon's formation. About 4.5 billion years ago, a Mars-sized body called Theia collided with the young Earth. The collision ejected a massive cloud of debris into orbit, which gradually coalesced to form the Moon. This theory explains why the Moon has a composition similar to Earth's mantle but a proportionally smaller iron core.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** Planetary Science / Lunar Formation Theory

### Question 10
What was the Mars-sized body that is believed to have collided with Earth to form the Moon?

A) Ceres
B) Theia
C) Vesta
D) Phaethon

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** The hypothetical Mars-sized body that struck the early Earth is called Theia, named after the Greek Titan who was the mother of Selene (the Moon goddess). Theia is thought to have been roughly the size of Mars — its catastrophic collision with Earth about 4.5 billion years ago produced the debris that eventually coalesced into the Moon.

**Difficulty:** Medium

### Question 11
What are the dark patches visible on the Moon from Earth called?

A) Craters
B) Maria (singular: Mare)
C) Highlands
D) Rilles

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** The dark patches visible on the Moon are called maria (singular: mare), from the Latin word for "sea." Early astronomers mistakenly thought they were bodies of water. Maria are actually flat basaltic plains formed by ancient volcanic lava flows billions of years ago. They are younger than the lighter-colored highlands and are concentrated mostly on the near side.

**Difficulty:** Easy

**Source:** Lunar Geology

### Question 12
What are the lighter, heavily cratered regions of the Moon called?

A) Maria
B) Regolith fields
C) Highlands (Terrae)
D) Basins

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** The lighter, heavily cratered regions of the Moon are called highlands or terrae. They are older than the maria and represent the Moon's original crust. The highlands appear lighter because they are made of anorthosite (a lighter-colored rock) compared to the dark basaltic rock of the maria. About 83% of the Moon's surface is highland terrain.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** Lunar Geology

### Question 13
Why are ancient impact craters preserved on the Moon for billions of years?

A) The Moon's strong magnetic field protects them
B) The Moon has no atmosphere, weather, or plate tectonics to erode them
C) The craters are made of an indestructible material
D) New impacts constantly refresh the craters

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** On Earth, craters are eroded by wind, rain, vegetation, and plate tectonics. The Moon has none of these — no atmosphere means no weather or wind erosion, no water means no water erosion, and no plate tectonics means no geological recycling of the surface. As a result, impact craters from billions of years ago remain essentially unchanged, making the Moon a geological time capsule.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** Lunar Geology

### Question 14
What is the South Pole-Aitken Basin?

A) A lake of liquid water at the Moon's south pole
B) The largest impact basin on the Moon, approximately 2,500 km in diameter
C) A volcanic region near the Moon's equator
D) The landing site of Apollo 11

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** The South Pole-Aitken Basin is the largest, deepest, and oldest recognised impact basin on the Moon — approximately 2,500 km in diameter and up to 8 km deep. It is also one of the largest impact structures in the entire solar system. Located on the Moon's far side, it is of great scientific interest because the impact may have excavated material from the Moon's mantle.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** Lunar Geology / NASA

### Question 15
The Moon does not produce its own light. How does it shine?

A) It generates light through nuclear fusion
B) It reflects sunlight
C) It glows due to radioactive minerals
D) Earth's light illuminates it

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** The Moon shines by reflecting sunlight — it has no internal light source. The Moon's surface reflects about 12% of the sunlight that hits it (its albedo is about 0.12). As the Moon orbits Earth, we see different portions of its sunlit surface from our perspective, which produces the familiar cycle of lunar phases.

**Difficulty:** Easy

**Source:** Astronomy

### Question 16
What is the correct order of the main lunar phases starting from New Moon?

A) New Moon → Full Moon → First Quarter → Last Quarter
B) New Moon → Waxing Crescent → First Quarter → Waxing Gibbous → Full Moon
C) Full Moon → Waning Crescent → New Moon → Waxing Crescent
D) New Moon → Last Quarter → Full Moon → First Quarter

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Starting from New Moon, the phases progress as: New Moon → Waxing Crescent → First Quarter (right half lit) → Waxing Gibbous → Full Moon → Waning Gibbous → Last Quarter (left half lit) → Waning Crescent → back to New Moon. "Waxing" means the illuminated portion is growing; "waning" means it is shrinking. The full cycle takes about 29.5 days.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** Astronomy

### Question 17
How long is one complete cycle of lunar phases (synodic period)?

A) About 24 hours
B) About 7 days
C) About 27.3 days
D) About 29.5 days

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** The synodic period (time from one New Moon to the next) is approximately 29.5 days. This is slightly longer than the Moon's orbital period around Earth (27.3 days, the sidereal period) because while the Moon orbits Earth, the Earth-Moon system is also orbiting the Sun. The extra ~2.2 days account for the additional angle the Moon must travel to return to the same Sun-Earth-Moon alignment.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** Astronomy / Celestial Mechanics

### Question 18
During a lunar eclipse, what happens?

A) The Moon passes between Earth and the Sun
B) Earth passes between the Sun and the Moon, casting its shadow on the Moon
C) The Sun passes between Earth and the Moon
D) The Moon enters the Sun's corona

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** During a lunar eclipse, Earth comes between the Sun and the Moon, and Earth's shadow falls on the Moon. The Moon can appear reddish during a total lunar eclipse (called a "Blood Moon") because Earth's atmosphere bends and filters sunlight, allowing red wavelengths to reach the Moon. Lunar eclipses are visible from the entire nightside of Earth.

**Difficulty:** Easy

**Source:** Astronomy / Eclipse Science

### Question 19
Why does the Moon sometimes appear reddish during a total lunar eclipse (a "Blood Moon")?

A) The Moon's surface contains red minerals
B) Earth's atmosphere bends and filters sunlight, allowing mainly red light to reach the Moon
C) Mars is close to the Moon during eclipses
D) The Moon heats up and glows red

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** During a total lunar eclipse, the Moon passes through Earth's shadow. However, Earth's atmosphere acts like a lens, bending (refracting) sunlight around the planet. Blue wavelengths are scattered away (the same reason sunsets are red), while red wavelengths pass through and illuminate the Moon, giving it a reddish or coppery colour — hence the name "Blood Moon."

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** Astronomy / Atmospheric Optics

### Question 20
Why don't eclipses happen every month?

A) The Moon's orbit is tilted about 5° to Earth's orbital plane, so alignment is rare
B) The Moon is too small to cause eclipses regularly
C) The Sun moves too fast for eclipses to occur monthly
D) Earth's atmosphere prevents eclipses most months

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** The Moon's orbital plane is tilted approximately 5° relative to Earth's orbital plane around the Sun (the ecliptic). Because of this tilt, the Moon usually passes slightly above or below the Sun-Earth line during its orbit. Eclipses only occur when the Moon crosses the ecliptic at the right time (at a "node"), which happens only 2-5 times per year.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** Astronomy / Celestial Mechanics

### Question 21
What was discovered in permanently shadowed craters near the Moon's poles?

A) Gold deposits
B) Water ice
C) Volcanic activity
D) Living organisms

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Water ice has been confirmed in permanently shadowed craters near the Moon's poles. These craters never receive direct sunlight, keeping temperatures below -150°C, allowing ice to persist for billions of years. This was first indicated by Chandrayaan-1's Moon Mineralogy Mapper (M3) instrument in 2008 and confirmed by subsequent missions. This water is invaluable for future exploration.

**Difficulty:** Easy

**Source:** ISRO Chandrayaan-1 / NASA

### Question 22
Why is water ice on the Moon so valuable for future space exploration?

A) It can be sold on Earth at high prices
B) It can be split into hydrogen (rocket fuel) and oxygen (breathing), and used as drinking water
C) It can be used to build structures on the Moon
D) It proves the Moon was once an ocean planet

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Lunar water ice is extremely valuable because through electrolysis (H₂O → H₂ + O₂), it can provide: drinking water for astronauts, breathable oxygen for life support, and hydrogen and oxygen for rocket fuel. Producing these resources on the Moon ("in-situ resource utilisation") would dramatically reduce the cost of lunar missions by eliminating the need to launch these heavy supplies from Earth.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** Space Exploration / ISRO / NASA

### Question 23
What are "peaks of eternal light" on the Moon?

A) Craters that always receive sunlight
B) Mountain tops near the poles that receive near-continuous sunlight
C) Bright reflective minerals on the equator
D) Artificial lights placed by Apollo missions

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Peaks of eternal light are elevated areas near the Moon's poles — particularly the south pole — that receive near-continuous sunlight due to the Moon's very slight axial tilt (~1.5°). While nearby craters are permanently in shadow, these mountaintops can receive sunlight for over 80-90% of the lunar year, making them ideal locations for solar-powered lunar bases.

**Difficulty:** Hard

**Source:** Lunar Exploration Science

### Question 30
The Moon's mass is approximately what fraction of Earth's mass?

A) 1/6
B) 1/12
C) 1/81
D) 1/333

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** The Moon's mass is approximately 1/81 of Earth's mass. Note that this is different from the gravity ratio: the Moon's surface gravity is about 1/6 of Earth's (not 1/81) because gravity depends on both mass AND radius. The Moon is smaller, so you are closer to its centre on the surface, which partially compensates for its lower mass.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** Planetary Science

### Question 31
Why is the Moon's surface gravity (1/6 of Earth) not proportional to its mass ratio (1/81 of Earth)?

A) The Moon has a stronger magnetic field that adds to gravity
B) Surface gravity depends on both mass and radius — the Moon is smaller, so you're closer to its centre
C) Tidal forces from Earth increase the Moon's gravity
D) The Moon's core is denser than Earth's core

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Surface gravity depends on mass divided by the square of the radius (g = GM/r²). Although the Moon has only 1/81 of Earth's mass, its radius is about 1/3.7 of Earth's. Being closer to the centre of mass means stronger gravity per unit mass. Working out the formula gives approximately 1/6 of Earth's surface gravity, not 1/81.

**Difficulty:** Hard

**Source:** Physics / Planetary Science

### Question 32
How old is the Moon?

A) About 1 billion years
B) About 3 billion years
C) About 4.5 billion years
D) About 13.8 billion years

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** The Moon is approximately 4.5 billion years old — roughly the same age as Earth and the rest of the solar system. This is consistent with the Giant Impact Hypothesis, which proposes the Moon formed from debris shortly after the formation of the early Earth. The age has been determined through radiometric dating of lunar rock samples returned by Apollo missions.

### Question 33
What is the temperature range on the Moon's surface?

A) 0°C to 50°C
B) -50°C to 100°C
C) -173°C to 127°C
D) -270°C to 300°C

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** The Moon's surface temperature ranges from approximately -173°C during the lunar night to about 127°C during the lunar day. This extreme 300°C range occurs because the Moon has virtually no atmosphere to regulate temperature — there is nothing to trap heat during the night or moderate it during the day, unlike Earth's atmosphere.

### Question 35
What distinguishes a solar eclipse from a lunar eclipse in terms of the Moon's position?

A) In a solar eclipse, Earth is between the Sun and Moon; in a lunar eclipse, the Moon is between Earth and Sun
B) In a solar eclipse, the Moon is between Earth and Sun; in a lunar eclipse, Earth is between the Sun and Moon
C) Both involve the Moon being between Earth and Sun
D) Both involve Earth being between the Sun and Moon

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** In a solar eclipse, the Moon passes between Earth and the Sun, blocking sunlight and casting its shadow on Earth. In a lunar eclipse, Earth passes between the Sun and the Moon, casting Earth's shadow on the Moon. The key difference is the Moon's position: between Earth and Sun (solar) versus behind Earth relative to Sun (lunar).

### Question 37
A student weighs 60 kg on Earth. Approximately how much would they weigh on the Moon?

A) 6 kg
B) 10 kg
C) 30 kg
D) 60 kg

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Since the Moon's gravity is approximately 1/6 of Earth's, a person who weighs 60 kg on Earth would weigh about 10 kg on the Moon (60 ÷ 6 = 10). Note that their mass remains 60 kg everywhere — mass doesn't change. What changes is the gravitational force (weight), which depends on the gravitational field strength of the body they're standing on.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** Physics

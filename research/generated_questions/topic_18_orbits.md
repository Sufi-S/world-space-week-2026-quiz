## Topic 18 – Orbits and Orbital Mechanics

### Questions for Topic 18

### Question 1
What is an orbit?

A) A straight line from Earth to the Sun
B) The curved path of an object around another object due to gravity
C) The speed at which a rocket launches
D) The distance between two planets

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** An orbit is the curved path that an object follows around another object due to the force of gravity. For example, the Moon orbits Earth and Earth orbits the Sun. A satellite in orbit is continuously falling toward Earth but moving forward fast enough that it keeps "missing" the ground — the Earth's surface curves away at the same rate the satellite falls.

**Difficulty:** Easy

**Source:** Orbital Mechanics / Basic Physics

### Question 2
What keeps a satellite in orbit around Earth?

A) Rocket engines that continuously fire
B) The balance between the satellite's forward velocity and Earth's gravitational pull
C) Magnetic forces from Earth's core
D) Air pressure pushing the satellite up

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** A satellite stays in orbit due to the balance between its forward velocity (tangential speed) and the gravitational pull of Earth. Gravity continuously pulls the satellite toward Earth, but the satellite is moving fast enough sideways that it keeps falling around Earth rather than into it. No continuous rocket thrust is needed once the satellite reaches the correct speed.

**Difficulty:** Easy

**Source:** Orbital Mechanics / Newtonian Physics

### Question 3
What is Low Earth Orbit (LEO)?

A) An orbit below 100 km altitude
B) An orbit at 200–2,000 km altitude
C) An orbit at exactly 35,786 km altitude
D) An orbit around the Moon

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Low Earth Orbit (LEO) is defined as the region between approximately 200 and 2,000 km altitude above Earth's surface. Satellites in LEO complete one orbit in roughly 90 minutes. The International Space Station (~400 km), Hubble Space Telescope (~547 km), and many Earth observation satellites operate in LEO.

**Difficulty:** Easy

**Source:** Orbital Mechanics

### Question 4
What altitude defines a Geostationary Earth Orbit (GEO)?

A) 400 km
B) 2,000 km
C) 20,200 km
D) 35,786 km

**Answer:** D

**Explanation:** A Geostationary Earth Orbit is located at exactly 35,786 km above Earth's equator. At this altitude, the orbital period is exactly 24 hours — matching Earth's rotation. This means a satellite in GEO appears to hover motionlessly over one fixed point on the equator, making it ideal for communications and weather monitoring.

**Difficulty:** Easy

**Source:** Orbital Mechanics

### Question 5
Why does a satellite in GEO appear stationary from the ground?

A) Because it is not actually moving
B) Because its orbital period matches Earth's 24-hour rotation
C) Because it is attached to a very tall tower
D) Because Earth's gravity stops it from moving

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** A GEO satellite orbits Earth once every 24 hours — exactly the same time Earth takes to rotate once. Since both the satellite and the ground below rotate at the same rate, the satellite appears to hover over a fixed point. It is actually moving at about 3 km/s (~11,000 km/h), but this motion exactly matches Earth's rotation.

**Difficulty:** Easy

**Source:** Orbital Mechanics

### Question 6
Who first proposed the concept of geostationary orbit for communications?

A) Isaac Newton in 1687
B) Konstantin Tsiolkovsky in 1903
C) Arthur C. Clarke in 1945
D) Wernher von Braun in 1952

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Science fiction writer and futurist Arthur C. Clarke proposed using geostationary orbit for telecommunications relay satellites in a 1945 paper in Wireless World magazine. He described how three satellites spaced 120° apart in GEO could provide worldwide radio coverage. For this reason, the geostationary orbit is sometimes called the "Clarke orbit."

### Question 7
What is the approximate orbital period of a satellite in LEO?

A) About 24 hours
B) About 12 hours
C) About 90 minutes
D) About 27 days

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** A satellite in Low Earth Orbit completes one orbit in approximately 90 minutes (about 1.5 hours). This is much faster than a GEO satellite (24 hours) because LEO satellites are much closer to Earth where gravity is stronger, requiring a higher orbital velocity (~7.7 km/s vs ~3 km/s for GEO).

**Difficulty:** Easy

### Question 8
What is the difference between a Geostationary (GEO) and a Geosynchronous (GSO) orbit?

A) There is no difference — they are the same
B) GEO is directly above the equator (0° inclination), while GSO can be at any inclination as long as the period is 24 hours
C) GSO is higher than GEO
D) GEO is used for military satellites, GSO for civilian

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Both GEO and GSO have 24-hour orbital periods. The difference is that GEO must be directly above the equator (0° inclination), making the satellite appear stationary from the ground. A GSO satellite can be inclined, meaning it traces a figure-8 pattern over the equator as seen from the ground. GEO is a special case of GSO with zero inclination.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** Orbital Mechanics

### Question 9
What is a polar orbit?

A) An orbit that passes over the North Pole only
B) An orbit with an inclination of approximately 90°, passing over or near both poles
C) An orbit around the polar ice caps
D) An orbit that only works in winter

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** A polar orbit has an inclination of approximately 90°, meaning the satellite passes over or near both the North and South Poles. As Earth rotates beneath the satellite, each successive orbit covers a different strip of the surface, eventually providing complete global coverage. Polar orbits are widely used for Earth observation and remote sensing.

**Difficulty:** Easy

**Source:** Orbital Mechanics

### Question 10
What is a Sun-Synchronous Orbit (SSO)?

A) An orbit that goes around the Sun
B) A special polar orbit that maintains a constant angle with the Sun, providing consistent lighting for imaging
C) An orbit that only passes over areas in sunlight
D) An orbit at the same distance as the Sun

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** A Sun-Synchronous Orbit (SSO) is a special type of near-polar orbit in which the orbital plane maintains a constant angle relative to the Sun. This means the satellite crosses the equator at the same local solar time on every pass, providing consistent lighting conditions — ideal for Earth observation and remote sensing. Typical SSO altitude is 600–800 km. ISRO's PSLV specialises in launching satellites to SSO.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** Orbital Mechanics / ISRO

### Question 12
What are perigee and apogee?

A) The start and end points of a rocket launch
B) The closest and farthest points from Earth in an elliptical orbit
C) Two types of rocket fuel
D) The north and south poles of a planet

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** In an elliptical orbit around Earth, the perigee is the point where the satellite is closest to Earth, and the apogee is the point where it is farthest from Earth. For orbits around the Sun, the equivalent terms are perihelion (closest) and aphelion (farthest). At perigee a satellite moves fastest; at apogee it moves slowest — a consequence of Kepler's second law.

**Difficulty:** Easy

**Source:** Orbital Mechanics

### Question 13
What are the five Lagrange Points?

A) Five locations on Earth where rockets can be launched
B) Five special positions in a two-body gravitational system where forces balance, allowing a small object to maintain a stable position
C) Five layers of Earth's atmosphere
D) Five stages of a rocket

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Lagrange points (L1–L5) are five positions in a two-body system (like Sun-Earth) where gravitational forces and orbital motion balance. L1 is between the two bodies (Aditya-L1 operates here), L2 is beyond the smaller body (JWST is here), L3 is on the opposite side of the larger body, and L4/L5 are 60° ahead and behind the smaller body (called Trojan points, and they are the most stable).

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** Orbital Mechanics / Celestial Mechanics

### Question 14
ISRO's Aditya-L1 operates at Lagrange Point L1. Where is this?

A) On the far side of the Moon
B) Between the Sun and Earth, about 1.5 million km from Earth
C) Beyond Earth, away from the Sun
D) At the centre of the asteroid belt

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** The Sun-Earth Lagrange Point L1 is located between the Sun and Earth, approximately 1.5 million km from Earth (about 1% of the Earth-Sun distance). At L1, the combined gravitational pull of the Sun and Earth, together with the centrifugal force of orbital motion, allows a spacecraft to maintain a stable position. This provides an uninterrupted view of the Sun without eclipses — ideal for solar observation.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** ISRO / Orbital Mechanics

### Question 15
The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) operates at which Lagrange Point?

A) L1
B) L2
C) L3
D) L5

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** JWST operates at the Sun-Earth Lagrange Point L2, approximately 1.5 million km from Earth in the direction away from the Sun. L2 is ideal for space telescopes because the spacecraft can keep the Sun, Earth, and Moon behind it (blocking their heat and light), while its sunshield protects its sensitive infrared instruments. Earth always stays nearby for communications.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** NASA / ESA / Orbital Mechanics

### Question 16
What is a Geostationary Transfer Orbit (GTO)?

A) An orbit used to transfer astronauts between space stations
B) An elliptical orbit with low perigee and apogee at GEO altitude, used to move satellites from LEO to GEO
C) A circular orbit halfway between LEO and GEO
D) An orbit that transfers between two planets

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** A Geostationary Transfer Orbit (GTO) is an elliptical orbit with a low perigee (~200–300 km) and an apogee at GEO altitude (~35,786 km). Rockets like GSLV and LVM3 place communication satellites into GTO. The satellite then fires its own onboard engine at apogee to circularise its orbit into GEO. GTO is the standard intermediate step for reaching geostationary orbit.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** Orbital Mechanics / ISRO

### Question 17
What is a Hohmann Transfer Orbit?

A) The fastest way to travel between two orbits
B) The most fuel-efficient way to move between two circular orbits using an elliptical transfer path
C) A special orbit used only for Moon missions
D) An orbit that requires no fuel at all

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** A Hohmann Transfer Orbit is the most fuel-efficient method of transferring between two circular orbits. It is an elliptical orbit that touches both the starting orbit (at perigee) and the destination orbit (at apogee). Two engine burns are required: one to enter the transfer orbit and one to circularise at the destination. While fuel-efficient, it is not the fastest transfer method.

**Difficulty:** Hard

**Source:** Orbital Mechanics

### Question 18
According to Kepler's First Law, what shape are planetary orbits?

A) Perfect circles
B) Ellipses with the Sun at one focus
C) Parabolas
D) Straight lines

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Kepler's First Law states that planets move in elliptical orbits with the Sun at one focus of the ellipse. While many planetary orbits are nearly circular, they are all technically ellipses. This was a revolutionary discovery by Johannes Kepler (published 1609), overturning the ancient belief that heavenly bodies must move in perfect circles.

**Difficulty:** Easy

**Source:** Kepler's Laws / Celestial Mechanics

### Question 19
Kepler's Second Law states that a planet sweeps equal areas in equal times. What does this mean in practice?

A) All planets move at the same speed
B) A planet moves faster when closer to the Sun and slower when farther away
C) Planets always move in straight lines
D) Larger planets orbit faster than smaller ones

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Kepler's Second Law (the equal areas law) means that a line drawn from the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas during equal intervals of time. In practice, this means a planet (or satellite) moves faster at perihelion (closest to Sun) and slower at aphelion (farthest from Sun). Similarly, a satellite in an elliptical orbit moves fastest at perigee and slowest at apogee.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** Kepler's Laws / Orbital Mechanics

### Question 20
What does Kepler's Third Law tell us about the relationship between orbital period and distance?

A) Larger orbits have shorter periods
B) The square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis (T² ∝ a³)
C) All orbits have the same period regardless of distance
D) Period is inversely proportional to distance

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Kepler's Third Law states that the square of a planet's orbital period (T²) is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis of its orbit (a³). This means that objects farther from the central body take much longer to complete an orbit. For example, Neptune (30 AU from the Sun) takes 165 years to orbit, while Earth (1 AU) takes just 1 year.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** Kepler's Laws / Orbital Mechanics

### Question 21
What is orbital inclination?

A) The speed of a satellite in orbit
B) The angle between the orbital plane and the equatorial plane
C) The height of the orbit above Earth's surface
D) The shape of the orbit

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Orbital inclination is the angle between the plane of a satellite's orbit and the equatorial plane of the body it orbits. An inclination of 0° means the orbit is directly above the equator (equatorial orbit, like GEO). An inclination of 90° is a polar orbit. An inclination greater than 90° indicates a retrograde orbit (the satellite orbits opposite to the body's rotation).

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** Orbital Mechanics

### Question 22
What is delta-v (Δv)?

A) The difference in volume between a rocket and its payload
B) The total change in velocity a rocket must provide for a mission or manoeuvre
C) The voltage used in satellite electronics
D) The speed of light minus the speed of the rocket

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Delta-v (Δv) is the total change in velocity that a rocket must provide to accomplish a particular mission or orbital manoeuvre. It is measured in km/s or m/s. More delta-v means more fuel is required. Going from Earth's surface to LEO requires about 9.4 km/s of Δv (including gravity and drag losses), making it the most energy-intensive part of most missions.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** Orbital Mechanics / Rocket Science

### Question 23
Approximately how much delta-v is needed to go from Earth's surface to Low Earth Orbit?

A) About 1 km/s
B) About 5 km/s
C) About 9.4 km/s
D) About 30 km/s

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Reaching LEO from Earth's surface requires approximately 9.4 km/s of delta-v. This includes not just the orbital velocity (~7.7 km/s at LEO altitude) but also additional velocity to overcome gravity losses during the vertical portion of the ascent and aerodynamic drag in the atmosphere. This enormous Δv requirement is why rockets must carry so much fuel.

**Difficulty:** Hard

**Source:** Orbital Mechanics / Rocket Science

### Question 24
Why do space agencies prefer to launch rockets from locations near the equator?

A) The weather is always clear at the equator
B) Earth's rotation provides a "free" velocity boost of up to ~465 m/s when launching eastward
C) The atmosphere is thinnest at the equator
D) There is less gravity at the equator

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Earth rotates at about 1,670 km/h (465 m/s) at the equator. When a rocket launches eastward from near the equator, it gets this rotational speed as a free velocity boost, reducing the fuel needed to reach orbit. Launching from higher latitudes provides less of this boost. This is why Sriharikota (13.7°N) was chosen for ISRO — it is one of the closest suitable coastal sites to the equator in India.

**Difficulty:** Medium

### Question 26
What happens to satellites in very low orbits over time due to atmospheric drag?

A) They speed up and move to higher orbits
B) They gradually lose altitude and eventually re-enter the atmosphere
C) They become permanently stable
D) They drift away from Earth

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** In very low orbits (below ~600 km), trace amounts of atmosphere create drag on satellites, slowly reducing their speed and altitude. Without periodic re-boost manoeuvres (like those performed on the ISS), the satellite's orbit decays until it re-enters the atmosphere and burns up. Current guidelines recommend satellites should de-orbit within 25 years of end of life to reduce space debris.

### Question 28

### Question 32
In Newton's thought experiment about orbital mechanics, what happens if you throw a ball horizontally from a very tall mountain fast enough?

A) The ball flies in a straight line forever
B) The ball falls to Earth in a parabolic arc
C) The ball falls around the Earth — it enters orbit
D) The ball escapes Earth's gravity entirely

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Newton imagined throwing a ball horizontally from an impossibly tall mountain. At low speed it falls to the ground nearby. Thrown faster, it lands farther away. At about 7.7 km/s (at LEO altitude), the ball falls toward Earth but the surface curves away at the same rate — the ball keeps falling around Earth and never hits the ground. It is now in orbit. This thought experiment elegantly illustrates orbital mechanics.

### Question 33
What is a halo orbit?

A) A perfectly circular orbit
B) An orbit around a Lagrange point rather than a planet or star
C) An orbit that produces a visible ring around a planet
D) An orbit only used by religious satellites

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** A halo orbit is a type of orbit in which a spacecraft orbits around a Lagrange point rather than sitting exactly at it. Both Aditya-L1 (at L1) and JWST (at L2) follow halo orbits — they trace periodic paths around their respective Lagrange points. Halo orbits are dynamically maintained with small thruster corrections, since L1 and L2 are unstable equilibrium points.

### Question 34
Why can a GEO orbit only exist directly above the equator?

A) Because the equator has the strongest gravity
B) Because only at the equator does a 24-hour orbit produce a stationary ground track (no north-south drift)
C) Because the atmosphere is thinnest at the equator
D) Because of magnetic field alignment

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** A geostationary orbit requires the satellite to appear motionless from the ground. This is only possible when the orbit is directly above the equator (0° inclination). If the orbit were inclined, the satellite would oscillate north and south of the equator once per orbit, tracing a figure-8 pattern — making it geosynchronous but not geostationary. Only an equatorial 24-hour orbit yields a fixed ground position.

**Difficulty:** Hard

**Source:** Orbital Mechanics

### Question 35

### Question 36

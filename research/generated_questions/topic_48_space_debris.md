## Topic 48 – Space Debris and Space Sustainability

### Questions for Topic 48

### Question 1
What is space debris?

A) Natural rocks floating in space (meteors and asteroids)
B) Non-functional human-made objects in Earth orbit — spent rocket stages, dead satellites, fragments, etc.
C) Dust from the Moon
D) Debris from the Sun

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Space debris (also called space junk or orbital debris) refers specifically to non-functional human-made objects in Earth orbit. This includes spent rocket stages, dead satellites, fragments from collisions and explosions, lost tools, paint flecks, and other artificial objects. Natural objects like meteoroids and asteroids are NOT classified as space debris.

**Difficulty:** Easy

**Source:** ESA / NASA

### Question 2
Why is even a tiny piece of space debris dangerous?

A) It's radioactive
B) Objects in LEO travel at 7-8 km/s, so even a 1 cm piece has the kinetic energy of a hand grenade
C) It's magnetic and attracts satellites
D) It blocks sunlight

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Objects in low Earth orbit travel at approximately 7-8 km/s (about 28,000 km/h). At these extreme speeds, even tiny debris is devastating. A 1 cm piece of debris has the kinetic energy of a hand grenade. A 10 cm fragment can hit with the force of 25 sticks of dynamite. Even a paint fleck can crack spacecraft windows — this has happened to Space Shuttle windows and ISS windows.

**Difficulty:** Easy

**Source:** ESA

### Question 3
Approximately how many tracked objects are in orbit around Earth?

A) About 100
B) About 1,000
C) About 45,900 regularly tracked objects (over 10 cm in size)
D) About 500

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** As of 2025-2026, approximately 45,900 objects larger than 10 cm are regularly tracked in Earth orbit. But this is just the tip of the iceberg: there are estimated to be about 900,000 objects between 1-10 cm and about 128 million pieces smaller than 1 cm. Of the tracked objects, only about 11,000 are active satellites — the rest is debris.

**Difficulty:** Easy

**Source:** ESA Space Debris Office

### Question 4
What is Kessler Syndrome?

A) A disease astronauts get in space
B) A theoretical cascade where collisions create more debris, leading to more collisions in a runaway chain reaction
C) A type of rocket engine
D) A method of tracking satellites

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Kessler Syndrome, proposed by NASA scientist Donald Kessler in 1978, describes a scenario where the density of objects in LEO becomes high enough that collisions between them create more fragments, which cause more collisions, leading to a self-sustaining cascade. This could make certain orbital altitudes unusable for generations. Some scientists believe certain LEO bands may already be approaching critical density.

**Difficulty:** Easy

**Source:** NASA

### Question 5
What was the largest single debris-creating event in space history?

A) The Cosmos-Iridium collision (2009)
B) China's deliberate destruction of the Fengyun-1C weather satellite (2007)
C) Mission Shakti (India, 2019)
D) The Columbia disaster (2003)

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** In January 2007, China conducted an anti-satellite (ASAT) test, deliberately destroying its own Fengyun-1C weather satellite at about 865 km altitude using a ground-launched missile. This was the largest single debris-creating event in history, producing over 2,300 tracked fragments, 35,000+ pieces larger than 1 cm, and roughly 1 million pieces larger than 1 mm. Many fragments will remain in orbit for decades.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** Space Debris Research

### Question 6
What was significant about the Cosmos-Iridium collision (2009)?

A) It was the first intentional satellite destruction
B) It was the first major accidental collision between two satellites in orbit
C) It destroyed the ISS
D) It was the first Indian satellite collision

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** In February 2009, the dead Russian satellite Kosmos-2251 collided with the active American communications satellite Iridium 33 at about 800 km altitude. Their relative speed was approximately 11.7 km/s. This was the first major accidental collision between two intact satellites, creating over 2,000 large debris fragments. It demonstrated that Kessler syndrome is a real and present risk.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** Space Debris Research

### Question 7
What was India's Mission Shakti (2019)?

A) A Moon landing mission
B) India's ASAT test — DRDO destroyed an Indian satellite, making India the 4th country with ASAT capability
C) A Mars orbiter mission
D) A satellite launch

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Mission Shakti (27 March 2019) was India's anti-satellite (ASAT) test, conducted by DRDO. A ground-based PDV Mark-II interceptor missile destroyed India's own Microsat-R satellite, demonstrating India's ability to neutralise satellites. India became the 4th country with ASAT capability (after USA, Russia, China). The test was deliberately conducted at low altitude (~300 km) so most debris would decay quickly through atmospheric drag.

**Difficulty:** Easy

**Source:** DRDO / ISRO

### Question 8
Why was Mission Shakti conducted at a low orbit (~300 km)?

A) Because the satellite was already at that altitude by accident
B) So that debris would decay quickly through atmospheric drag — most re-entered within weeks/months
C) Because higher orbits are illegal for ASAT tests
D) Because the missile couldn't reach higher

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** India deliberately tested at approximately 300 km altitude so that the resulting debris would be affected by atmospheric drag and re-enter Earth's atmosphere quickly, burning up harmlessly. About 270 debris pieces were tracked, and most re-entered within weeks to months. This was a responsible approach compared to China's 2007 test at 865 km, where debris will persist for decades.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** DRDO

### Question 9
What is the "25-year rule" for space debris?

A) Satellites must be replaced every 25 years
B) Guidelines recommend that LEO satellites de-orbit within 25 years of their mission ending
C) Debris must be cleaned up within 25 years
D) Space missions can last a maximum of 25 years

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** International guidelines recommend that satellites in LEO should be de-orbited (removed from orbit) within 25 years of their mission ending. This limits the accumulation of dead satellites in orbit. However, many experts and newer proposals suggest this should be reduced to just 5 years. The 25-year guideline is voluntary, not legally binding, and compliance is still incomplete.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** IADC / UN

### Question 10
What happens to old geostationary satellites at end of life?

A) They are brought back to Earth
B) They are boosted about 300 km above GEO to a "graveyard orbit"
C) They are destroyed with lasers
D) They just stay where they are forever

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** When geostationary orbit (GEO) satellites reach end of life, responsible operators boost them about 300 km above the GEO belt into a "graveyard orbit" (also called supersynchronous or disposal orbit). This clears the valuable GEO slot for new satellites. Since GEO is at about 36,000 km, there's minimal atmospheric drag, so satellites there would never naturally de-orbit.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** Space Industry

### Question 11
What is ISRO's IS4OM?

A) A rocket engine
B) ISRO System for Safe and Sustainable Space Operations Management — India's debris tracking and collision avoidance network
C) A type of satellite
D) A space station module

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** IS4OM (ISRO System for Safe and Sustainable Space Operations Management) is India's dedicated centre for tracking space debris and providing collision avoidance warnings to ISRO satellites. It monitors space objects, calculates conjunction (close approach) risks, and recommends avoidance manoeuvres to protect India's active satellites from debris collisions.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** ISRO

### Question 12
What are Whipple shields?

A) Heat shields for re-entry
B) Multi-layer debris protection used on the ISS to protect against small impacts
C) Solar panel covers
D) Communication antenna guards

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Whipple shields are multi-layer protective barriers used on the ISS and other spacecraft to protect against small debris impacts. Named after astronomer Fred Whipple, they work by using a thin outer "bumper" layer spaced away from the main wall. When debris hits the bumper, it shatters into smaller fragments that spread out before hitting the inner wall, distributing the impact energy.

**Difficulty:** Hard

**Source:** NASA

### Question 13
Which countries have demonstrated anti-satellite (ASAT) capability?

A) Only USA and Russia
B) USA, Russia, China, and India — in that order
C) All countries with satellites
D) Only China and India

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Four countries have demonstrated ASAT capability through kinetic (physical destruction) tests: the USA (first tested in 1985), Russia/USSR (various tests), China (2007 Fengyun-1C test), and India (2019 Mission Shakti). In 2022, the US announced a moratorium on destructive ASAT testing and called on other nations to follow, recognising the debris problem these tests create.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** Space Policy

### Question 14
What is "passivation" in the context of space debris mitigation?

A) Making a satellite invisible to radar
B) At end of life, draining remaining fuel and depressurising tanks to prevent explosions
C) Shutting off a satellite's solar panels
D) Painting a satellite to reduce visibility

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Passivation is a key debris mitigation practice where, at end of life, a spacecraft's remaining fuel is drained or vented and its tanks and batteries are depressurised. This prevents accidental explosions, which are actually one of the largest sources of space debris. A single exploding rocket upper stage can create hundreds of fragments. About half of all debris events have been caused by such explosions.

**Difficulty:** Hard

**Source:** IADC

### Question 15
What is active debris removal (ADR)?

A) Using satellites to photograph debris
B) Proposed technologies to physically remove debris from orbit — using nets, harpoons, robotic arms, or lasers
C) Dodging debris during launch
D) Tracking debris with radar

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Active debris removal (ADR) refers to proposed technologies that would physically remove debris from orbit. Concepts include deploying nets or harpoons to capture debris, using robotic arms to grab and de-orbit objects, and firing lasers to alter orbits. ESA's ClearSpace-1 is planned as the first active debris removal mission. No operational ADR mission has been completed yet — it remains one of the biggest technical challenges in space.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** ESA

### Question 16
What is a "conjunction" in space debris terminology?

A) Two planets aligning
B) A close approach between two objects in orbit that may risk collision
C) A rocket launch
D) A satellite entering orbit

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** In space operations, a "conjunction" is a close approach between two objects in orbit — a potential collision risk. Space surveillance networks track objects and issue conjunction warnings when two objects are predicted to pass dangerously close. Active satellites can then perform collision avoidance manoeuvres to dodge. The ISS regularly performs such manoeuvres, sometimes with only hours of warning.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** Space Operations

### Question 17
Roughly what fraction of all Mars missions have failed?

A) Almost none
B) About one-quarter
C) About half
D) About three-quarters

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Roughly half of all Mars missions have failed — this is known as the "Mars Curse." Mars is exceptionally difficult to reach and land on due to the long journey time (6-9 months), thin atmosphere, communication delays, and countless things that can go wrong. India's MOM/Mangalyaan was especially impressive because it succeeded on its very first attempt.

**Difficulty:** Easy

**Source:** Space History

### Question 18
What did Russia's destruction of Kosmos-1408 (November 2021) cause?

A) No significant debris
B) Over 1,500 trackable debris pieces, forcing the ISS crew to shelter in their capsules
C) The end of Russia's space programme
D) A collision with the Moon

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** In November 2021, Russia destroyed its old Kosmos-1408 satellite at about 450 km altitude, creating over 1,500 trackable debris pieces. The debris cloud passed close enough to the ISS that crew members had to shelter in their Soyuz and Crew Dragon capsules as a precaution. The test was widely condemned internationally, including by NASA, as reckless.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** NASA / Space News

### Question 19
What is the estimated total mass of all human-made objects currently in Earth orbit?

A) About 100 kg
B) About 8,000+ metric tonnes
C) About 100 metric tonnes
D) About 1 million metric tonnes

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** The total mass of all human-made objects in Earth orbit — including active satellites, dead satellites, rocket stages, and debris — is estimated at over 8,000 metric tonnes. This mass continues to grow as more satellites and constellations (like SpaceX's Starlink) are launched. Managing this growing mass is one of the key challenges for space sustainability.

**Difficulty:** Hard

**Source:** ESA Space Debris Office

### Question 20
What is ESA's ClearSpace-1?

A) A weather satellite
B) Planned as the first active debris removal mission — designed to capture and de-orbit a piece of debris
C) A communication satellite
D) A space station module

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** ClearSpace-1 is an ESA-led mission planned to be the first active debris removal demonstration. It aims to use robotic arms to capture a Vega Secondary Payload Adapter (a piece of rocket debris) and de-orbit it so it burns up in the atmosphere. If successful, it would prove that removing debris from orbit is feasible, paving the way for operational cleanup missions.

**Difficulty:** Hard

**Source:** ESA

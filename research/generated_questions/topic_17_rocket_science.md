## Topic 17 – Rocket Science Fundamentals

### Questions for Topic 17

### Question 1
Which fundamental law of physics explains how rockets work?

A) Newton's First Law of Motion
B) Newton's Second Law of Motion
C) Newton's Third Law of Motion
D) Law of Conservation of Energy

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Rockets work on Newton's Third Law of Motion: "For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction." The rocket engine expels hot gases downward at high speed (action), and the rocket is pushed upward in the opposite direction (reaction). This principle works in vacuum too — rockets don't need air to push against.

**Difficulty:** Easy

**Source:** Physics / Rocket Propulsion Fundamentals

### Question 2
What makes a rocket different from a jet engine?

A) Rockets are smaller than jet engines
B) Rockets carry both fuel and oxidiser, while jets take oxygen from the atmosphere
C) Rockets use electricity while jets use fuel
D) There is no difference

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** A rocket carries everything it needs for propulsion — both fuel and oxidiser — making it the only type of engine that can work in the vacuum of space. A jet engine takes oxygen from the surrounding atmosphere, which is why jets cannot fly in space. This self-contained propulsion is the defining characteristic of a rocket.

### Question 3
Who is known as the "Father of Astronautics" for publishing the rocket equation in 1903?

A) Robert Goddard
B) Wernher von Braun
C) Konstantin Tsiolkovsky
D) Hermann Oberth

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Konstantin Tsiolkovsky (1857–1935), a Russian scientist, is called the "Father of Astronautics." He published the fundamental rocket equation in 1903 — the same year as the Wright Brothers' first flight. His equation showed that a rocket's final speed depends on the exhaust velocity and the ratio of initial mass (with fuel) to final mass (without fuel).

### Question 6
What is a "propellant" in rocket terminology?

A) Only the fuel
B) Only the oxidiser
C) The combination of fuel and oxidiser together
D) The rocket's exhaust gases

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** In rocketry, "propellant" refers to the complete combination of fuel and oxidiser. A rocket needs both because there is no oxygen in space. The fuel provides the chemical energy, while the oxidiser provides the oxygen needed for combustion. Common propellant combinations include UDMH + N₂O₄ (liquid), and aluminium powder + ammonium perchlorate (solid).

**Difficulty:** Easy

**Source:** Rocket Propulsion Fundamentals

### Question 7
What type of rocket propellant is pre-mixed into a solid compound and cannot be shut down once ignited?

A) Liquid propellant
B) Solid propellant
C) Cryogenic propellant
D) Ion propellant

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Solid propellant rockets have fuel and oxidiser pre-mixed into a solid compound — like a giant firework. Once ignited, they burn continuously until all propellant is consumed and cannot be shut down or throttled. Their advantages are simplicity, reliability, and long storage life. Examples include the PSLV's first stage (S139) and LVM3's S200 strap-on boosters.

**Difficulty:** Easy

**Source:** Rocket Propulsion / ISRO

### Question 8
What is the key advantage of liquid propellant rockets over solid propellant rockets?

A) They are cheaper to build
B) They can be throttled, shut down, and restarted
C) They produce more smoke
D) They are lighter

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Liquid propellant rockets store fuel and oxidiser separately as liquids, which are pumped into a combustion chamber. This allows precise control — the engine can be throttled (adjusted), shut down, and even restarted during flight. This controllability is essential for orbital manoeuvres and precision landings. Solid rockets, once lit, burn until empty with no control.

**Difficulty:** Easy

**Source:** Rocket Propulsion Fundamentals

### Question 9
What does "cryogenic" mean in the context of rocket engines?

A) Extremely hot
B) Extremely cold
C) Extremely pressurised
D) Extremely lightweight

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** "Cryogenic" comes from the Greek word "cryo" meaning extremely cold. Cryogenic rocket engines use propellants that must be stored at extremely low temperatures: Liquid Hydrogen (LH₂) at -253°C and Liquid Oxygen (LOX) at -183°C. Despite the handling challenges, cryogenic engines are the most efficient chemical rocket engines, producing only water as exhaust.

**Difficulty:** Easy

**Source:** Rocket Propulsion Fundamentals

### Question 10
What propellant combination do cryogenic rocket engines use?

A) Kerosene + Liquid Oxygen
B) Liquid Hydrogen + Liquid Oxygen
C) UDMH + N₂O₄
D) Solid aluminium + ammonium perchlorate

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Cryogenic engines use Liquid Hydrogen (LH₂) as fuel and Liquid Oxygen (LOX) as oxidiser. This combination produces the highest specific impulse (efficiency) of any chemical rocket — about 450 seconds. The exhaust product is simply water (H₂ + O₂ → H₂O). The challenge is that both propellants must be stored at extremely low temperatures (-253°C for LH₂, -183°C for LOX).

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** Rocket Propulsion / ISRO

### Question 11
What is Specific Impulse (Isp)?

A) The speed of a rocket at launch
B) The total weight of the rocket
C) A measure of how efficiently a rocket engine uses propellant
D) The maximum altitude a rocket can reach

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Specific Impulse (Isp) measures how efficiently a rocket engine uses its propellant — specifically, the thrust produced per unit weight of propellant consumed per second. It is measured in seconds. A higher Isp means a more efficient engine. Typical values: solid rockets ~250 s, liquid engines ~290 s, cryogenic ~450 s, and ion engines ~3,000+ s.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** Rocket Propulsion Fundamentals

### Question 12
Which type of rocket engine has the highest Specific Impulse (Isp) among chemical rockets?

A) Solid propellant (~250 s)
B) Liquid propellant using UDMH/N₂O₄ (~290 s)
C) Cryogenic using LH₂/LOX (~450 s)
D) All chemical engines have the same Isp

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** Among chemical rocket engines, cryogenic engines using Liquid Hydrogen and Liquid Oxygen have the highest specific impulse at approximately 450 seconds. This is because the extremely light hydrogen molecules, when heated and expelled at high velocity, produce the most efficient thrust per unit of propellant mass. This efficiency is why cryogenic upper stages are used for heavy payloads and deep-space missions.

**Difficulty:** Medium

### Question 13
Why do rockets use multiple stages instead of one large stage?

A) To make the rocket look taller
B) To drop empty fuel tanks and reduce mass, allowing remaining stages to accelerate more efficiently
C) Because no single engine can burn for more than 60 seconds
D) To carry more passengers

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Multi-stage rockets discard empty fuel tanks and engines as each stage runs out of propellant. Shedding this "dead weight" dramatically improves the remaining rocket's thrust-to-weight ratio, allowing it to accelerate more efficiently. A single-stage rocket would have to carry all the empty tank mass all the way to orbit, wasting enormous amounts of fuel. Most rockets use 2–4 stages.

### Question 16
What is the "payload" of a rocket?

A) The total fuel carried by the rocket
B) The satellite or spacecraft the rocket carries to space
C) The total weight of the rocket at launch
D) The thrust produced by the engines

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** The payload is the cargo — the satellite, spacecraft, or other equipment — that the rocket is designed to deliver to its target orbit or destination. Everything else (fuel, engines, tanks, fairings) exists solely to get the payload where it needs to go. The payload fairing is the protective nose cone that shields the payload during atmospheric flight and is jettisoned at about 100–115 km altitude.

### Question 17
What is a payload fairing and when is it jettisoned?

A) A protective nose cone that shields the payload; jettisoned once above the dense atmosphere (~100–115 km)
B) A fuel tank that is dropped after first-stage separation
C) The engine nozzle that is ejected after launch
D) A heat shield that stays on throughout the mission

**Answer:** A

**Explanation:** The payload fairing is the aerodynamic nose cone at the top of the rocket that protects the satellite from aerodynamic forces, heating, and acoustic vibrations during atmospheric flight. Once the rocket reaches an altitude of about 100–115 km where the atmosphere is too thin to cause damage, the fairing is split and jettisoned to reduce weight.

**Difficulty:** Medium

### Question 18
For a rocket to lift off the ground, its thrust-to-weight ratio must be:

A) Exactly 1
B) Less than 1
C) Greater than 1
D) It doesn't matter

**Answer:** C

**Explanation:** A rocket can only lift off if its engines produce more thrust than the rocket's total weight — meaning the thrust-to-weight ratio must be greater than 1. If the ratio is exactly 1, the rocket hovers. If less than 1, it doesn't leave the ground. The Saturn V, for example, produced 34.5 MN of thrust at liftoff to lift its 2,970-tonne launch mass.

**Difficulty:** Medium

**Source:** Rocket Propulsion Fundamentals

### Question 22
What does "hypergolic" mean in rocket propulsion?

A) Propellants that must be ignited with a spark
B) Propellants that ignite spontaneously on contact with each other
C) Propellants that burn extremely slowly
D) Propellants that only work in vacuum

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Hypergolic propellants are fuel-oxidiser combinations that ignite spontaneously the moment they come into contact — no ignition system needed. The most common hypergolic pair is UDMH + N₂O₄, used in the Vikas engine. Hypergolic propellants are valued for their reliability (guaranteed ignition) and restartability, but they tend to be toxic and corrosive, requiring careful handling.

### Question 27
What is ion (electric) propulsion, and what is its main advantage?

A) It uses nuclear reactions; its advantage is enormous thrust
B) It uses electric fields to accelerate charged particles; it has very high fuel efficiency but very low thrust
C) It uses magnets to push against Earth's magnetic field
D) It uses compressed air; its advantage is simplicity

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** Ion propulsion uses electric fields to accelerate ions (charged particles) to extremely high velocities. Its specific impulse can exceed 3,000 seconds — far higher than any chemical rocket. However, it produces very low thrust (millinewtons), so it cannot lift a rocket off a planet. It is ideal for deep space missions and satellite station-keeping where gentle continuous acceleration over time is effective. Examples include NASA's Dawn and JAXA's Hayabusa missions.

### Question 29
What is the purpose of a turbopump in a liquid rocket engine?

A) To cool the rocket nozzle
B) To steer the rocket

D) To generate electricity for the satellite

**Answer:** C

### Question 30
In the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation, a rocket's final velocity depends on:

A) Only the total mass of fuel
B) The exhaust velocity and the ratio of initial mass to final mass
C) The colour of the rocket
D) Only the number of stages

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** The Tsiolkovsky rocket equation (Δv = ve × ln(m₀/mf)) shows that a rocket's change in velocity depends on two factors: (1) the exhaust velocity (ve) — how fast gases leave the engine, and (2) the mass ratio — the ratio of the rocket's initial mass (with fuel, m₀) to its final mass (without fuel, mf). This is why rockets must carry enormous amounts of fuel and why staging is essential.

**Difficulty:** Hard

**Source:** Rocket Propulsion / Tsiolkovsky

### Question 42
A rocket's nozzle is bell-shaped. What is the purpose of this design?

A) To look aerodynamic
B) To direct and accelerate exhaust gases efficiently, converting thermal energy to kinetic energy
C) To collect air for combustion
D) To reduce the rocket's weight

**Answer:** B

**Explanation:** The bell-shaped (convergent-divergent or de Laval) nozzle is critical for rocket performance. Hot, high-pressure gases from the combustion chamber enter the narrow throat (convergent section), accelerate to supersonic speeds, then expand further in the wider exit (divergent section). This shape efficiently converts the thermal energy of combustion gases into directed kinetic energy, maximising thrust.

**Source:** Rocket Propulsion Fundamentals / Thermodynamics

# Topic 25: Indian Launch Vehicles — PSLV

### Learn This Topic

#### Overview
The **Polar Satellite Launch Vehicle (PSLV)** is ISRO's most reliable and versatile rocket, often called the **"Workhorse of ISRO"**. It has launched over 50 successful missions, carrying satellites for India and dozens of other countries.

#### Development History
- Developed by **VSSC** (Vikram Sarabhai Space Centre), Thiruvananthapuram
- First flight: **PSLV-D1** (September 20, 1993) — **failed** (attitude control problem in second stage)
- Second flight: **PSLV-D2** (October 15, 1994) — **success!** Launched IRS-P2 satellite
- Since then, PSLV has had an extraordinary success record with only one other partial failure (PSLV-C39, 2017, payload fairing issue)

#### Design — Four Stages (Alternating Solid-Liquid)
PSLV uses an innovative **alternating solid-liquid-solid-liquid** stage configuration:

| Stage | Type | Propellant | Details |
|-------|------|-----------|---------|
| **Stage 1 (PS1)** | Solid | HTPB (Hydroxyl-Terminated Polybutadiene) | S139 motor — one of the largest solid rocket motors in the world. 139 tonnes of propellant. Burns for ~105 seconds |
| **Stage 2 (PS2)** | Liquid | UDMH + N₂O₄ | Vikas engine (derived from French Viking engine). ~40 tonnes of propellant. Provides controlled, throttleable thrust |
| **Stage 3 (PS3)** | Solid | HTPB | HPS3 motor. ~7.6 tonnes. Upper atmosphere boost |
| **Stage 4 (PS4)** | Liquid | MMH + MON-3 | Two restartable engines. ~2.5 tonnes. Final orbit insertion. Can restart multiple times for precise orbit placement |

Plus **6 strap-on boosters** around Stage 1 (in PSLV-XL variant): solid motors, each with 12.2 tonnes of propellant

#### PSLV Variants
| Variant | Strap-on Boosters | Payload to SSO (600 km) | Notable Missions |
|---------|-------------------|------------------------|------------------|
| **PSLV-G** (Generic) | 6 × 9-tonne PSOM | ~1,050 kg | Early PSLV missions |
| **PSLV-CA** (Core Alone) | None (no strap-ons) | ~1,100 kg (to LEO) | Lighter payloads, Chandrayaan-1 used PSLV-XL not CA |
| **PSLV-XL** (eXtra Large) | 6 × 12.2-tonne PSOM-XL | ~1,750 kg | Chandrayaan-1, MOM, Aditya-L1, most missions |
| **PSLV-DL** (Dual) | 2 × PSOM-XL | ~1,250 kg | Intermediate capability |
| **PSLV-QL** (Quad) | 4 × PSOM-XL | ~1,500 kg | Intermediate capability |

#### Record-Breaking Missions
- **PSLV-C37** (February 15, 2017): Launched **104 satellites** in a single mission — a **world record** at the time! (Cartosat-2 series + 103 co-passengers from 6 countries)
- **PSLV-C11** (October 22, 2008): Launched **Chandrayaan-1** to the Moon
- **PSLV-C25** (November 5, 2013): Launched **Mars Orbiter Mission (Mangalyaan)** — India's first interplanetary mission
- **PSLV-C57** (September 2, 2023): Launched **Aditya-L1** to study the Sun
- **PSLV-C58** (January 1, 2024): Launched **XPoSat**
- **PSLV-C60** (December 30, 2024): Launched **SpaDeX** docking experiment

#### POEM — PSLV Orbital Experimental Module
- After the main payload is deployed, the spent PS4 (4th stage) has residual fuel
- ISRO converted this into **POEM** — an orbital platform for experiments
- PS4 stage orientates itself, provides power via solar panels, and hosts payloads
- First POEM: PSLV-C53 (2022). Subsequent missions have continued using POEM.
- Turns "space junk" into a useful orbital lab — very innovative!

#### Commercial Launches
- PSLV has launched satellites for many countries including USA, UK, Germany, France, Canada, Japan, South Korea, Israel, Singapore, and many more
- Over 400 foreign satellites launched commercially
- Revenue earned through Antrix Corporation and NSIL

#### Why Called "Workhorse of ISRO"
- Most reliable Indian launch vehicle
- Over 50 successful missions
- Versatile — launches to LEO, SSO, GTO (using higher energy configurations), and even lunar/interplanetary transfer orbits
- Affordable and consistently available
- The backbone of India's launch capability

### Key Facts
- First successful flight: PSLV-D2, October 15, 1994
- Four stages: Solid → Liquid → Solid → Liquid (alternating)
- PSLV-XL is the most commonly used variant
- World record: 104 satellites in one launch (PSLV-C37, Feb 2017)
- Launched Chandrayaan-1, MOM, Aditya-L1, XPoSat, SpaDeX
- Uses the Vikas engine in its second stage
- Stage 1 (S139) is one of the world's largest solid rocket motors
- PS4 stage repurposed as POEM orbital platform

### Important Terms
- **PSOM**: PSLV Strap-On Motor
- **HTPB**: Hydroxyl-Terminated Polybutadiene (solid propellant)
- **Vikas engine**: Liquid engine used in PSLV 2nd stage and GSLV core
- **POEM**: PSLV Orbital Experimental Module
- **SSO**: Sun-Synchronous Orbit (PSLV's primary target orbit)

### Common Confusions
| Confusion | Clarification |
|-----------|---------------|
| PSLV can only launch to polar orbits | Despite the name, PSLV launches to many orbits — SSO, LEO, GTO, and even interplanetary trajectories |
| PSLV has never failed | PSLV-D1 (1993) failed, and PSLV-C39 (2017) had a partial failure. But the overall record is excellent |
| All PSLV variants are the same | There are 5 variants (G, CA, XL, DL, QL) with different strap-on configurations |
| PSLV uses cryogenic engines | No — PSLV uses solid and storable liquid propellants, NOT cryogenic. GSLV/LVM3 use cryogenic upper stages |

# Topic 26: Indian Launch Vehicles — GSLV and LVM3

### Learn This Topic

#### GSLV — Geosynchronous Satellite Launch Vehicle

**Purpose**: Designed to launch heavier satellites (2,000-2,500 kg) to **Geostationary Transfer Orbit (GTO)** — primarily for communication satellites that need to reach GEO.

**The Cryogenic Engine Story — India's Determination**
- To launch heavy communication satellites to GEO, India needed a rocket with a **cryogenic upper stage** (LH₂/LOX — most efficient chemical propulsion)
- In 1991, ISRO signed a deal with Russia's **Glavkosmos** for cryogenic engines AND technology transfer
- The **United States** intervened, pressuring Russia under the **MTCR** (Missile Technology Control Regime)
- Russia was forced to cancel the **technology transfer** — they delivered only **7 ready-made KVD-1 engines** but no manufacturing know-how
- ISRO was left with no choice but to **develop its own cryogenic engine from scratch**
- This took nearly **20 years** of intense effort by LPSC (Liquid Propulsion Systems Centre)
- Result: the **CE-7.5** cryogenic engine
- **GSLV-D5** (January 5, 2014): First successful flight with indigenous cryogenic engine — a landmark achievement!

**GSLV Design — Three Stages**
| Stage | Type | Details |
|-------|------|---------|
| **Stage 1** | Solid (S139) + 4 liquid strap-on boosters (L40, each with Vikas engine) | Same solid core as PSLV Stage 1, plus 4 liquid boosters |
| **Stage 2 (GS2)** | Liquid | Vikas engine, similar to PSLV PS2 |
| **Stage 3 (CUS)** | Cryogenic Upper Stage | LH₂ + LOX, CE-7.5 engine (indigenous) or KVD-1 (Russian, in early flights) |

**GSLV Variants**
- **GSLV Mk I**: Used Russian KVD-1 cryogenic engine. Early flights (GSLV-D1, D2, F01, F02, F04) — mixed success record
- **GSLV Mk II**: Uses **indigenous CE-7.5** cryogenic engine. First success: GSLV-D5 (Jan 2014). More reliable.
- Payload to GTO: ~2,500 kg

#### LVM3 (Launch Vehicle Mark-3) — India's Heaviest Rocket

**Previously called GSLV Mk III** — renamed to **LVM3** because it is a completely different vehicle, not just an upgrade of GSLV.

**Purpose**: India's heavy-lift launch vehicle. Can place ~4,000 kg to GTO or ~8,000 kg to LEO. Needed for heavy communication satellites (previously launched on foreign rockets like Ariane 5) and for Gaganyaan (human spaceflight).

**LVM3 Design — Three-Stage Vehicle**
| Component | Type | Details |
|-----------|------|---------|
| **S200** | 2 × Solid strap-on boosters | Largest solid boosters developed by ISRO. 200 tonnes of propellant each! |
| **L110** | Liquid core stage | 2 × Vikas engines. 110 tonnes of propellant (UDMH + N₂O₄) |
| **C25** | Cryogenic upper stage | CE-20 engine — India's most powerful cryogenic engine. 25 tonnes of LH₂/LOX |

**Development Timeline**
| Date | Mission | Significance |
|------|---------|-------------|
| Dec 18, 2014 | **LVM3-X/CARE** | Experimental suborbital flight. Tested Crew Module (CARE) atmospheric re-entry — relevant to Gaganyaan |
| June 5, 2017 | **GSLV MkIII-D1** | First orbital flight. Launched GSAT-19 (3,136 kg) to GTO |
| Nov 14, 2018 | **GSLV MkIII-D2** | Launched GSAT-29 (3,423 kg) |
| July 22, 2019 | **GSLV MkIII-M1** | Launched **Chandrayaan-2** (~3,850 kg) |
| Oct 23, 2022 | **LVM3-M2** | First **commercial** mission — launched 36 **OneWeb** internet satellites (~5,796 kg to LEO) |
| March 26, 2023 | **LVM3-M3** | Second OneWeb launch (36 satellites) |
| July 14, 2023 | **LVM3-M4** | Launched **Chandrayaan-3** |

**CE-20 Cryogenic Engine**
- Developed by LPSC
- Uses **Gas Generator cycle** (unlike CE-7.5 which uses staged combustion)
- Thrust: ~200 kN (about 2.7× more powerful than CE-7.5)
- Uses LH₂/LOX
- Powers the C25 upper stage of LVM3

#### Comparison Table: PSLV vs GSLV vs LVM3

| Feature | PSLV | GSLV Mk II | LVM3 |
|---------|------|-----------|------|
| **Height** | ~44 m | ~49 m | ~43 m |
| **Liftoff Mass** | ~320 tonnes (XL) | ~414 tonnes | ~640 tonnes |
| **Stages** | 4 (S-L-S-L) | 3 (S+L strap-ons, L, Cryo) | 3 (S strap-ons, L, Cryo) |
| **Payload to LEO** | ~1,750 kg | ~5,000 kg | ~8,000 kg |
| **Payload to GTO** | ~1,425 kg | ~2,500 kg | ~4,000 kg |
| **Cryogenic Stage** | No | Yes (CE-7.5) | Yes (CE-20) |
| **First Flight** | 1993 | 2010 (with CE-7.5: 2014) | 2017 |
| **Primary Use** | SSO, LEO, interplanetary | GTO (communication sats) | GTO, LEO, human spaceflight |

### Key Facts
- GSLV uses indigenous CE-7.5 cryogenic engine (first success: GSLV-D5, Jan 5, 2014)
- LVM3 is India's heaviest rocket — can carry ~4,000 kg to GTO
- LVM3 was formerly called GSLV Mk III
- LVM3 uses CE-20 cryogenic engine (more powerful than CE-7.5)
- S200 solid boosters on LVM3 carry 200 tonnes of propellant each
- LVM3 launched Chandrayaan-2, Chandrayaan-3, and OneWeb commercial missions
- India's cryogenic engine development took ~20 years after technology denial
- LVM3 will be human-rated for Gaganyaan

### Important Terms
- **GTO**: Geostationary Transfer Orbit
- **CUS**: Cryogenic Upper Stage
- **CE-7.5**: Indigenous cryogenic engine for GSLV Mk II (7.5 kN vacuum thrust in older designation, actually ~73.5 kN)
- **CE-20**: More powerful cryogenic engine for LVM3 (~200 kN)
- **S200**: Solid strap-on booster for LVM3 (200 tonnes propellant)
- **L110**: Liquid core stage of LVM3 (110 tonnes propellant)
- **C25**: Cryogenic upper stage of LVM3 (25 tonnes propellant)
- **MTCR**: Missile Technology Control Regime
- **KVD-1**: Russian cryogenic engine (used in early GSLV flights)

### Common Confusions
| Confusion | Clarification |
|-----------|---------------|
| GSLV Mk III is an upgrade of GSLV | LVM3 (formerly GSLV Mk III) is a completely different vehicle with different design |
| India bought cryogenic technology from Russia | Russia was forced to cancel tech transfer; India developed its own |
| PSLV and GSLV use the same cryogenic engine | PSLV has NO cryogenic stage at all. GSLV Mk II uses CE-7.5, LVM3 uses CE-20 |
| LVM3 is only for communication satellites | It's also used for lunar missions (Chandrayaan-2/3), commercial LEO launches (OneWeb), and will be used for human spaceflight (Gaganyaan) |

# Topic 27: Indian Launch Vehicles — SLV, ASLV, SSLV and Sounding Rockets

### Learn This Topic

#### India's Rocket Evolution
India's journey in rocketry followed a systematic step-by-step approach: sounding rockets → SLV → ASLV → PSLV → GSLV → LVM3 → SSLV. Each step built expertise for the next.

#### Sounding Rockets — Where It All Began

**What is a sounding rocket?**
- A sub-orbital rocket designed to carry scientific instruments to the upper atmosphere (30-200+ km)
- Does NOT reach orbit — goes up, takes measurements, comes back down
- Called "sounding" from the nautical term "to sound" (meaning to take measurements)

**India's Sounding Rocket Programme**
| Year | Rocket | Details |
|------|--------|---------|
| **Nov 21, 1963** | **Nike-Apache** | First sounding rocket from Thumba. American-made. Parts carried on bicycle! |
| 1965-67 | Various foreign rockets | Continued experiments with foreign sounding rockets |
| **1967** | **Rohini-75 (RH-75)** | First Indian-made sounding rocket. Named after a star (Rohini = Aldebaran) |
| 1970s-present | **RH-200, RH-300, RH-560** | Progressively larger Indian sounding rockets |

**Rohini Sounding Rocket Family**
- **RH-75**: 75 mm diameter, smallest
- **RH-200**: 200 mm diameter, most widely used
- **RH-300**: 300 mm diameter (Mk II variant used for meteorological studies)
- **RH-560**: 560 mm diameter, can reach ~550 km altitude

Sounding rockets are still launched regularly from Thumba for atmospheric and meteorological research.

#### SLV-3 — Satellite Launch Vehicle-3 (India's First Orbital Rocket)

- **Project Director**: **APJ Abdul Kalam**
- India's first indigenous satellite launch vehicle
- Goal: place a 40 kg satellite into LEO (~400 km)
- **Four-stage, all-solid-propellant** rocket
- Height: ~22 m, Mass: ~17 tonnes

| Flight | Date | Result |
|--------|------|--------|
| **SLV-3 E1** | August 10, 1979 | **Failed** — control system malfunction. Satish Dhawan took the blame publicly. |
| **SLV-3 E2** | **July 18, 1980** | **SUCCESS!** Launched **Rohini RS-1** satellite (35 kg) into orbit. India became the **6th nation** to independently launch a satellite! Satish Dhawan pushed Kalam to face the press. |
| SLV-3 D1 | May 31, 1981 | Success — launched Rohini RS-D1 |
| SLV-3 D2 | April 17, 1983 | Success — launched Rohini RS-D2 |

**Significance**: Proved India could design, build, and operate a satellite launch vehicle entirely on its own. Built foundational expertise for all future rockets.

#### ASLV — Augmented Satellite Launch Vehicle

- An augmented version of SLV-3 with **2 strap-on boosters** (using SLV-3 first stages as strap-ons)
- Goal: place 150 kg into LEO (~400 km)
- Five stages
- Height: ~24 m, Mass: ~40 tonnes

| Flight | Date | Result |
|--------|------|--------|
| ASLV-D1 | March 24, 1987 | Failed |
| ASLV-D2 | July 13, 1988 | Failed |
| ASLV-D3 | May 20, 1992 | Success — launched SROSS-C |
| ASLV-D4 | May 4, 1994 | Success — launched SROSS-C2 |

**Significance**: ASLV had a troubled development with multiple failures, but it served as a critical **learning experience**. Technologies like strap-on boosters, improved guidance systems, and larger solid motors — all lessons from ASLV — were crucial for PSLV's success.

#### SSLV — Small Satellite Launch Vehicle (India's Newest Rocket)

- **Newest addition** to India's launch vehicle family
- Designed for **quick, affordable launches of small satellites** (up to 500 kg to LEO)
- **Three stages, all solid propellant** + a liquid-propulsion-based Velocity Trimming Module (VTM) for precise orbit insertion
- Height: ~34 m, Mass: ~120 tonnes
- Can be assembled in **72 hours** (vs weeks for PSLV) — "launch on demand" capability
- Minimal launch infrastructure needed — uses the new Third Launch Pad at SDSC SHAR
- Aimed at the growing **small satellite market**

| Flight | Date | Result |
|--------|------|--------|
| **SSLV-D1** | August 7, 2022 | **Partial failure** — satellites placed in wrong orbit (elliptical instead of circular), decayed |
| **SSLV-D2** | **February 10, 2023** | **Success!** Launched EOS-07, Janus-1, and AzaadiSAT-2 |

**Significance**: Opens up dedicated small satellite launches. India can now offer quick, affordable launch services for the booming small-sat industry.

#### RLV-TD — Reusable Launch Vehicle Technology Demonstrator

- ISRO is developing **reusable launch vehicle** technology for the future
- **RLV-TD** (Technology Demonstrator): A winged body that looks like a mini space shuttle
- First test: **May 23, 2016** — HEX (Hypersonic Flight Experiment). RLV-TD launched to 65 km altitude, re-entered atmosphere, and splashed down in Bay of Bengal. Successful!
- **LEX (Landing Experiment)**: April 2, 2023 — RLV-TD was dropped from a helicopter at 4.5 km altitude and autonomously landed on a runway at the Aeronautical Test Range, Chitradurga, Karnataka. Successful!
- Further tests planned: powered flight + autonomous landing
- Future goal: Significantly reduce launch costs through reusability

#### NGLV — Next Generation Launch Vehicle
- Under development — India's future heavy-lift rocket
- Planned to replace PSLV and LVM3 eventually
- Expected to use semi-cryogenic engines (LOX + kerosene)
- Reusability features planned
- Payload: ~10,000 kg to GTO (much more than LVM3)

### Key Facts
- First sounding rocket from Thumba: November 21, 1963 (Nike-Apache)
- RH-75 (1967) was the first Indian-made sounding rocket
- SLV-3 success (July 18, 1980) made India the 6th nation with launch capability
- APJ Abdul Kalam was SLV-3 project director
- ASLV failures provided crucial lessons for PSLV
- SSLV: newest rocket, all-solid, quick assembly (72 hours), small satellite market
- RLV-TD demonstrated autonomous landing in 2023
- India's rocket journey: bicycle + bullock cart → PSLV/LVM3

### Important Terms
- **Sounding rocket**: Sub-orbital rocket for atmospheric research
- **SLV-3**: Satellite Launch Vehicle-3 (India's first orbital rocket)
- **ASLV**: Augmented Satellite Launch Vehicle
- **SSLV**: Small Satellite Launch Vehicle
- **RLV-TD**: Reusable Launch Vehicle Technology Demonstrator
- **VTM**: Velocity Trimming Module (on SSLV)
- **NGLV**: Next Generation Launch Vehicle
- **Rohini**: Name given to India's sounding rockets and early satellites

### Common Confusions
| Confusion | Clarification |
|-----------|---------------|
| India's first rocket was SLV-3 | SLV-3 was the first orbital rocket. India launched sounding rockets from 1963, 17 years earlier |
| SLV-3 succeeded on its first try | First attempt (E1, 1979) failed. Success came on the second attempt (E2, 1980) |
| ASLV was a failure | It had failures, but these provided crucial learning. Technologies from ASLV fed into PSLV |
| SSLV is meant to replace PSLV | SSLV is complementary — for small satellites. PSLV handles medium payloads. Different markets |
| RLV means India has a reusable rocket now | RLV-TD is a technology demonstrator. A fully operational reusable launch vehicle is still years away |

# Topic 28: Satish Dhawan Space Centre, Sriharikota (SDSC SHAR)

### Learn This Topic

#### Location and Geography
- Located on **Sriharikota island** in **Nellore district, Andhra Pradesh**
- Situated between **Pulicat Lake** (India's second-largest brackish water lake) to the west and the **Bay of Bengal** to the east
- Latitude: approximately **13.7°N** (relatively close to the equator — important for launch advantage)
- About 80 km north of Chennai

#### Why Sriharikota Was Chosen
1. **Near the equator**: Earth's rotational velocity provides a "free" speed boost for eastward launches (~465 m/s at equator, ~450 m/s at Sriharikota)
2. **East coast location**: Rockets launch eastward over the Bay of Bengal — no populated land masses underneath the flight path (safety)
3. **Island location**: Isolated area, good for safety exclusion zones
4. **Proximity to Chennai/Bengaluru**: Logistically accessible from major cities and ISRO centres

#### History
- Established in **1971** as **Sriharikota Range (SHAR)**
- First launch from SHAR: Rohini sounding rocket (October 9, 1971)
- First satellite launch: **SLV-3** (August 10, 1979 — the failed attempt; success on July 18, 1980)
- **Renamed** to **Satish Dhawan Space Centre** in **2002** after the passing of Prof. Satish Dhawan

#### Who Was Satish Dhawan?
- **Full name**: Satish Dhawan
- **Born**: September 25, 1920, Srinagar
- **Died**: January 3, 2002, Bengaluru
- ISRO Chairman: **1972–1984**
- Also Director of **IISc (Indian Institute of Science), Bengaluru** — held both positions simultaneously!
- Pioneered **experimental fluid dynamics** in India — built India's first supersonic wind tunnels
- Under his leadership: SLV-3 programme, INSAT system, IRS programme
- **Famous leadership story** (repeated because it's THAT important for quizzes):
  - When SLV-3 E1 failed (1979): Dhawan personally faced the press and took full responsibility
  - When SLV-3 E2 succeeded (1980): Dhawan asked APJ Abdul Kalam (project director) to face the press
  - Lesson: A true leader takes blame for failure and gives credit for success

#### Launch Facilities at SDSC SHAR

**Launch Pads**
1. **First Launch Pad (FLP)**: Used for PSLV launches. Operational since SLV-3 era.
2. **Second Launch Pad (SLP)**: Used for GSLV and LVM3 launches. Commissioned in 2005. Also used for some PSLV launches.
3. **Third Launch Pad (TLP)**: Newest — built specifically for **SSLV** (Small Satellite Launch Vehicle). Simpler infrastructure for quick-turnaround launches.

**Other Facilities**
- **Vehicle Assembly Building (VAB)**: Where rockets are assembled vertically
- **Mobile Service Tower**: Provides access to the rocket on the launch pad for payload integration
- **Solid Propellant Space Booster Plant (SPROB)**: Manufactures and processes solid rocket propellant
- **Static Test and Evaluation Complex (STEX)**: Tests rocket motors before they fly
- **Mission Control Centre**: Monitors and controls launches
- **Rocket motor processing and fuel filling facilities**
- **Tracking stations and radar systems**
- **Recovery and safety infrastructure**

#### All Indian Orbital Launches Are from SDSC SHAR
Every Indian satellite launched by an Indian rocket has lifted off from Sriharikota:
- All SLV-3 flights
- All ASLV flights
- All PSLV flights
- All GSLV flights
- All LVM3 flights
- All SSLV flights

There is no other Indian orbital launch site (though discussions about a second spaceport in Tamil Nadu's Kulasekarapattinam have been ongoing).

### Key Facts
- Located on Sriharikota island, Andhra Pradesh, between Pulicat Lake and Bay of Bengal
- Latitude: ~13.7°N (near equator for launch advantage)
- Named after Satish Dhawan in 2002
- India's only operational spaceport — ALL Indian orbital launches happen here
- Has 3 launch pads: FLP (PSLV), SLP (GSLV/LVM3), TLP (SSLV)
- East coast location allows safe launches over the sea
- Established 1971, first satellite launch attempt 1979

### Important Terms
- **SDSC SHAR**: Satish Dhawan Space Centre, Sriharikota Range
- **FLP/SLP/TLP**: First/Second/Third Launch Pad
- **VAB**: Vehicle Assembly Building
- **SPROB**: Solid Propellant Space Booster Plant
- **Pulicat Lake**: Brackish water lagoon to the west of Sriharikota

### Common Confusions
| Confusion | Clarification |
|-----------|---------------|
| Thumba is India's main launch site | Thumba (Kerala) is for sounding rockets. All orbital launches are from Sriharikota (AP) |
| Sriharikota is on the west coast | It's on the EAST coast — Bay of Bengal side. East coast is chosen so rockets fly over the sea |
| SDSC SHAR was always named after Satish Dhawan | It was originally called "Sriharikota Range (SHAR)". Renamed in 2002 |
| India can only launch from Sriharikota | Currently yes for orbital launches, but a second spaceport (Kulasekarapattinam, TN) is being developed |
| Being near the equator doesn't matter | It does! The equator gives maximum rotational velocity boost (~465 m/s), reducing fuel needed |

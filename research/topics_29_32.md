# Topic 29: Indian Communication Satellites (INSAT/GSAT/CMS)

### Learn This Topic

#### The INSAT System — India's Communication Backbone
**INSAT (Indian National Satellite System)** is one of the **largest domestic communication satellite systems** in the Asia-Pacific region. It serves telecommunications, television broadcasting, weather monitoring, and search-and-rescue.

#### Evolution of Indian Communication Satellites

**APPLE — The Beginning (1981)**
- **Ariane Passenger Payload Experiment** — India's first experimental communication satellite
- Launched on June 19, 1981, by ESA's Ariane-1 rocket from French Guiana
- Weighed only 670 kg
- Famous photograph: APPLE being transported on a **bullock cart** in Bengaluru for testing!
- Successfully demonstrated satellite-based TV broadcasting and communication

**INSAT-1 Series (1983–1990)**
- India's first operational communication satellite system
- **INSAT-1A** (1982): Launched but failed within months
- **INSAT-1B** (1983): India's first successful operational multipurpose satellite — **communication + meteorology** in one satellite
- INSAT-1C, INSAT-1D followed
- Built by Ford Aerospace (USA) — India had not yet developed indigenous capability for heavy GEO satellites

**INSAT-2 Series (1992–1999)**
- **First fully indigenous** multipurpose Indian communication satellites
- **INSAT-2A** (1992): First Indian-built communication satellite in GEO
- Design, development, and fabrication at ISAC (now URSC), Bengaluru
- Provided TV broadcasting, telecommunications, meteorological services

**INSAT-3 Series (2000–2007)**
- Improved capabilities — more transponders, better meteorological payloads
- INSAT-3B, 3C, 3A, 3E, 3D (dedicated weather satellite)
- INSAT-3DR: meteorological satellite with atmospheric sounding

**INSAT-4 Series (2005–2011)**
- High-power communication satellites
- INSAT-4A, 4B, 4CR
- Direct-to-Home (DTH) TV broadcasting capability
- Launched on GSLV and foreign rockets (Ariane 5)

**GSAT Series (2001–2020s)**
- **GSAT (Geosynchronous Satellite)** — dedicated communication satellites
- Over 20 GSAT satellites launched
- **GSAT-11** (2018): India's **heaviest** communication satellite (~5,854 kg), launched on Ariane 5. High-throughput satellite for broadband internet
- **GSAT-7**: Dedicated military communication satellite (Indian Navy)
- **GSAT-7A**: Dedicated satellite for Indian Air Force
- GSAT-14, GSAT-6A, GSAT-29, GSAT-30, GSAT-31, etc.

**CMS Series (2020s–present)**
- New naming convention: **CMS (Communication Satellite)**
- **CMS-01** (2020): Communication satellite replacing GSAT-12, launched by PSLV
- **CMS-02** (planned)
- Continuation of India's communication satellite programme under new naming

#### Applications of Communication Satellites
- **Television broadcasting**: National channels (Doordarshan), DTH services, educational TV
- **Telecommunications**: Voice, data, and internet connectivity, especially in remote/rural areas
- **VSAT (Very Small Aperture Terminal)**: Business communications, banking networks
- **Telemedicine**: Connecting rural health centres to urban hospitals via satellite
- **Tele-education**: Reaching remote schools through satellite-based educational programmes
- **Disaster Warning**: Cyclone warning dissemination through satellite-based systems
- **Search and Rescue**: INSAT carries payloads for locating distress signals

#### Geostationary Orbit for Communication
- Communication satellites are placed in **GEO (35,786 km)** so they appear stationary
- Ground antennas can point at a fixed spot — no tracking needed
- One GEO satellite can cover about 1/3 of Earth's surface
- Three GEO satellites can cover most of the globe (except polar regions)

### Key Facts
- INSAT is one of the largest domestic communication satellite systems in Asia-Pacific
- APPLE (1981) was India's first communication satellite experiment — bullock cart photo!
- INSAT-1B (1983) was the first operational multipurpose satellite
- INSAT-2A (1992) was the first fully indigenous communication satellite
- GSAT-11 is India's heaviest communication satellite (~5,854 kg)
- Communication satellites are placed in GEO (35,786 km)
- Applications: TV, telecom, internet, telemedicine, tele-education, disaster warning

### Important Terms
- **INSAT**: Indian National Satellite System
- **GSAT**: Geosynchronous Satellite
- **CMS**: Communication Satellite (new naming)
- **GEO**: Geostationary Earth Orbit
- **DTH**: Direct-to-Home (television)
- **VSAT**: Very Small Aperture Terminal
- **Transponder**: Device on a satellite that receives, amplifies, and retransmits signals

### Common Confusions
| Confusion | Clarification |
|-----------|---------------|
| INSAT satellites are only for communication | INSAT is multipurpose — communication AND meteorology in same satellite |
| All Indian communication satellites are Indian-built | INSAT-1 series was built by Ford Aerospace, USA. Indigenous building started with INSAT-2A (1992) |
| GSAT and INSAT are separate systems | GSAT satellites are part of/supplement the INSAT system |
| India can launch its own heavy GEO satellites | Most heavy GEO satellites were launched by Ariane 5. GSLV/LVM3 can now handle some, but the heaviest still need foreign rockets |

# Topic 30: Indian Earth Observation Satellites (IRS/EOS)

### Learn This Topic

#### Overview
India operates one of the **largest constellations of Earth observation/remote sensing satellites** in the world. These satellites look DOWN at Earth, providing crucial data for agriculture, forestry, water resources, urban planning, disaster management, and defence.

#### Evolution

**IRS Series (Indian Remote Sensing)**
| Satellite | Year | Significance |
|-----------|------|-------------|
| **IRS-1A** | 1988 | India's **first operational remote sensing satellite** (launched by USSR's Vostok rocket) |
| **IRS-1B** | 1991 | Improved version |
| **IRS-1C** | 1995 | Better resolution, launched by Russian Molniya rocket |
| **IRS-1D** | 1997 | Launched by India's own PSLV |
| **IRS-P2** | 1994 | First satellite launched by PSLV (PSLV-D2 mission) |
| **IRS-P3** | 1996 | Multi-sensor satellite |
| **IRS-P4 (Oceansat-1)** | 1999 | Oceanography |
| **IRS-P5 (Cartosat-1)** | 2005 | Cartography (stereo imaging) |
| **IRS-P6 (Resourcesat-1)** | 2003 | Natural resources monitoring |

**Modern Earth Observation Satellites**

**Cartosat Series** — High-resolution cartographic (mapping) satellites:
- Cartosat-1 (2005): Stereo imaging for terrain mapping
- Cartosat-2 series (2007-2018): Very high resolution (~0.65 m), near real-time imaging
- Cartosat-3 (2019): Sub-metre resolution (~0.25 m panchromatic) — among the best in the world

**Resourcesat Series** — Natural resources monitoring:
- Resourcesat-1 (2003), Resourcesat-2 (2011), Resourcesat-2A (2016)
- Monitor agriculture, forests, water bodies, minerals

**Oceansat Series** — Ocean studies:
- Oceansat-1 (1999), Oceansat-2 (2009), Oceansat-3/EOS-06 (2022)
- Ocean colour, sea surface temperature, wind speed
- Helps identify potential fishing zones (PFZ) — directly helps fishermen

**RISAT Series** — Radar Imaging Satellites:
- **RISAT-2** (2009): Used Israeli-built radar, all-weather/day-night imaging capability
- **RISAT-1** (2012): India's first indigenous SAR (Synthetic Aperture Radar) satellite
- RISAT-2B (2019), RISAT-2BR1 (2019)
- SAR can image through clouds and in darkness — critical for disaster monitoring and military

**HySIS** — Hyper Spectral Imaging Satellite (2018):
- Can image in 55 spectral bands — very detailed compositional analysis
- Applications: agriculture (crop health), mining, environmental monitoring

**EOS (Earth Observation Satellite) — New Naming Convention**
- ISRO transitioned to EOS naming:
- **EOS-01** (2020): Radar imaging satellite (RISAT successor)
- **EOS-02** (on SSLV-D1, 2022 — placed in wrong orbit)
- **EOS-03**: Geo-imaging satellite (GISAT-1) — launch failed (GSLV-F10, 2021)
- **EOS-04** (2022): Radar imaging satellite
- **EOS-06** (2022): Oceansat-3 — ocean and atmospheric studies
- **EOS-07** (2023): Small Earth observation satellite (on SSLV-D2)
- **EOS-08** (2024): Microsatellite with thermal and UV imaging

**NISAR — NASA-ISRO SAR**
- Joint mission between NASA and ISRO
- **Dual-frequency SAR**: L-band (NASA/JPL) + S-band (ISRO)
- Will map the entire Earth every 12 days
- Applications: monitoring ecosystems, ice sheets, earthquakes, volcanoes, groundwater
- One of the most advanced Earth observation satellites ever built
- Launch: 2024 on GSLV [VERIFY if launched by Oct 2026]

#### Applications of Earth Observation
- **Agriculture**: Crop monitoring, acreage estimation, drought assessment, soil moisture
- **Water Resources**: Groundwater prospects, watershed mapping, reservoir monitoring
- **Forestry**: Forest cover mapping, deforestation monitoring, biodiversity
- **Disaster Management**: Flood mapping, cyclone damage assessment, earthquake impact, landslide monitoring
- **Urban Planning**: City mapping, infrastructure monitoring, encroachment detection
- **Coastal Zone Management**: Shoreline change, mangrove mapping
- **Defence and Security**: Surveillance, border monitoring
- **Cartography/Mapping**: High-resolution maps of India

### Key Facts
- IRS-1A (1988) was India's first remote sensing satellite
- India has one of the world's largest constellations of Earth observation satellites
- Cartosat-3 achieves sub-metre resolution (~0.25 m)
- RISAT uses Synthetic Aperture Radar — works through clouds and at night
- NISAR is a joint NASA-ISRO mission with dual-frequency radar
- These satellites directly help farmers, fishermen, disaster response, and defence

### Important Terms
- **IRS**: Indian Remote Sensing satellite series
- **EOS**: Earth Observation Satellite (new naming)
- **SAR**: Synthetic Aperture Radar (images through clouds/night)
- **Panchromatic**: Black-and-white imaging (higher resolution)
- **Multispectral**: Multiple colour bands (for compositional analysis)
- **Hyperspectral**: Many narrow spectral bands (very detailed composition)
- **PFZ**: Potential Fishing Zone (identified using satellite data)

### Common Confusions
| Confusion | Clarification |
|-----------|---------------|
| Earth observation satellites look outward at space | They look DOWN at Earth — cameras and sensors pointed at the planet's surface |
| India only has a few EO satellites | India has one of the world's largest constellations — dozens of operational EO satellites |
| Radar satellites are the same as optical | Radar (SAR) uses microwave signals, not light. Can see through clouds and in darkness. Optical needs sunlight and clear skies |
| IRS and EOS are different programmes | EOS is just the new naming convention for India's Earth observation satellites |

# Topic 31: NavIC — Indian Regional Navigation Satellite System

### Learn This Topic

#### What Is NavIC?
**NavIC (Navigation with Indian Constellation)** is India's own **regional satellite navigation system**, similar to GPS but designed to cover India and its surrounding region. It was previously known as **IRNSS (Indian Regional Navigation Satellite System)**.

The name "NavIC" also means "navigator" or "sailor" in Hindi/Sanskrit. The name was coined by PM Narendra Modi.

#### Why Did India Build Its Own Navigation System?
- During the **Kargil War (1999)**, India requested GPS data from the United States for tracking Pakistani intrusions
- The **USA denied** India access to accurate GPS signals
- This was a critical wake-up call — India could not rely on foreign navigation systems for national security
- ISRO was tasked with building an **indigenous navigation system**
- This is another example of India's self-reliance philosophy: when denied technology, India builds its own

#### Constellation Design
- **7 satellites** in total:
  - **3 in GEO** (Geostationary Earth Orbit, 35,786 km, over the equator at specific longitudes)
  - **4 in inclined GSO** (Geosynchronous orbit inclined at 29° to the equator)
- This combination ensures that **all 7 satellites are always visible from India** — unlike GPS where satellites come and go overhead

#### Coverage Area
- Primary coverage: **India** and a region extending approximately **1,500 km around India** (roughly 30°S to 50°N latitude, 30°E to 130°E longitude)
- This covers India, its neighbourhood, and surrounding seas — the Indian Ocean Region
- It is **regional**, not global (unlike GPS, GLONASS, Galileo, and BeiDou which are global)

#### Satellites in the NavIC Constellation
| Satellite | Launch Date | Orbit | Launcher |
|-----------|------------|-------|----------|
| IRNSS-1A | July 1, 2013 | Inclined GSO | PSLV-C22 |
| IRNSS-1B | April 4, 2014 | Inclined GSO | PSLV-C24 |
| IRNSS-1C | October 16, 2014 | GEO | PSLV-C26 |
| IRNSS-1D | March 28, 2015 | Inclined GSO | PSLV-C27 |
| IRNSS-1E | January 20, 2016 | Inclined GSO | PSLV-C31 |
| IRNSS-1F | March 10, 2016 | GEO | PSLV-C32 |
| IRNSS-1G | April 28, 2016 | GEO | PSLV-C33 |

Later additions: **NVS-01** (May 2023, replacement satellite, launched by GSLV), **NVS-02** (2024/25, PSLV) — these replenish the constellation as older satellites age.

#### Two Types of Service
1. **SPS (Standard Positioning Service)**: Open, civilian use. Accuracy: ~5 metres
2. **RS (Restricted Service)**: Encrypted, for authorised users (military, security forces). Higher accuracy: ~0.1 metres

#### How Navigation Satellites Work
- Each satellite continuously broadcasts its position and a very precise time signal (atomic clocks on board)
- A receiver on the ground picks up signals from multiple satellites
- By measuring the time difference of signals from at least 4 satellites, the receiver calculates its position (latitude, longitude, altitude) and time — this is called **trilateration**
- More satellites in view = better accuracy

#### Comparison with Global Navigation Systems
| System | Country | Satellites | Coverage | Operational |
|--------|---------|-----------|----------|-------------|
| **GPS** | USA | ~31 | Global | 1995 |
| **GLONASS** | Russia | ~24 | Global | 1993 (restored 2011) |
| **Galileo** | EU (ESA) | ~30 | Global | 2016 (initial), 2024 (full) |
| **BeiDou** | China | ~35 | Global (BDS-3) | 2020 |
| **NavIC (IRNSS)** | India | 7 | Regional (India + 1500 km) | 2018 |
| **QZSS** | Japan | 4 | Regional (Asia-Pacific) | 2018 |

#### Applications
- **Terrestrial navigation**: Vehicle tracking, fleet management, personal navigation
- **Maritime navigation**: Ship tracking, fishing vessel management
- **Aviation**: Air navigation services
- **Disaster management**: Precise location of affected areas, rescue coordination
- **Mapping and surveying**: Geodetic surveys, urban mapping
- **Precision timing**: Banking, telecom, power grid synchronisation
- **Defence**: Military operations, precision targeting

### Key Facts
- NavIC was built after the Kargil War GPS denial by the USA
- 7 satellites: 3 GEO + 4 inclined GSO
- Coverage: India + 1,500 km surrounding region (regional, not global)
- Two services: SPS (civilian, ~5 m accuracy) and RS (restricted, ~0.1 m)
- All NavIC satellites launched by PSLV (except NVS-01 by GSLV)
- Full constellation operational since 2018
- "NavIC" means navigator in Hindi/Sanskrit, named by PM Modi

### Important Terms
- **NavIC**: Navigation with Indian Constellation
- **IRNSS**: Indian Regional Navigation Satellite System (earlier name)
- **SPS**: Standard Positioning Service (open, civilian)
- **RS**: Restricted Service (encrypted, military)
- **Trilateration**: Method of determining position using distances from known points
- **NVS**: Navigation Satellite (replenishment series)

### Common Confusions
| Confusion | Clarification |
|-----------|---------------|
| NavIC is India's GPS | NavIC is REGIONAL (India + 1500 km), GPS is GLOBAL. They serve similar purposes but different coverage areas |
| NavIC has 24+ satellites like GPS | NavIC uses only 7 satellites (combination of GEO + GSO ensures visibility over India) |
| NavIC was created to replace GPS in India | NavIC complements GPS, doesn't replace it. Most phones in India still use GPS, but NavIC support is being added |
| All NavIC satellites are in the same orbit | 3 are in GEO (equatorial) and 4 are in inclined GSO — different orbit types |

# Topic 32: Indian Meteorological and Scientific Satellites

### Learn This Topic

#### Weather/Meteorological Satellites

**INSAT Meteorological Payloads**
- Many INSAT communication satellites carried **weather monitoring payloads**
- These provided **VHRR (Very High Resolution Radiometer)** images for weather forecasting
- India's weather monitoring started as a "secondary payload" on communication satellites

**Kalpana-1 (2002)**
- India's first dedicated **exclusive meteorological satellite**
- Originally named MetSat-1, renamed **Kalpana-1** in memory of **Kalpana Chawla** after the Columbia disaster (2003)
- Launched: September 12, 2002, by PSLV-C4
- Placed in GEO
- Carried VHRR for weather monitoring, Data Relay Transponder (DRT)

**INSAT-3D (2013) and INSAT-3DR (2016)**
- Dedicated advanced meteorological satellites
- **INSAT-3D**: Launched July 2013, carries 6-channel imager, 19-channel sounder (atmospheric profiling), DRT, Satellite Aided Search and Rescue (SAS&R)
- **INSAT-3DR**: Launched September 2016, improved version of INSAT-3D
- Provide data for: weather forecasting, cyclone tracking, fog detection, severe weather alerts

#### Joint Meteorological/Oceanographic Missions

**Megha-Tropiques (2011)**
- **Indo-French joint mission** (ISRO + CNES)
- Launched: October 12, 2011, by PSLV-C18
- Studies **tropical weather** and climate — water cycle, energy budget in the tropics
- Low-inclination orbit (20°) — spends most time over tropics
- "Megha" means "cloud" in Sanskrit, "Tropiques" means "tropics" in French
- Instruments: MADRAS (microwave radiometer), SAPHIR (humidity profiler), ScaRaB (radiation budget), ROSA (GPS radio occultation)

**SARAL (2013)**
- **Satellite with ARgos and ALtika** — Indo-French joint mission (ISRO + CNES)
- Launched: February 25, 2013, by PSLV-C20
- Carries AltiKa (Ka-band altimeter) for ocean surface height measurement
- Applications: ocean circulation, sea level monitoring, coastal zone management

**Oceansat Series**
- **Oceansat-1** (IRS-P4, 1999): Ocean colour monitor for biological productivity, sediment distribution
- **Oceansat-2** (2009): Ocean colour, sea surface winds (scatterometer)
- **Oceansat-3/EOS-06** (2022): Improved ocean and atmospheric observations, including ARGOS for tracking

**SCATSAT-1 (2016)**
- Scatterometer satellite — measures ocean surface wind speed and direction
- Critical for weather forecasting and cyclone prediction
- Launched by PSLV-C35

#### How Indian Weather Satellites Save Lives
India has dramatically improved its **cyclone prediction** capability thanks to weather satellites:
- In 1999, a super cyclone in Odisha killed ~10,000 people (poor warning systems)
- By 2013 (Cyclone Phailin), advance satellite-based tracking and warnings led to massive evacuations — casualties reduced to 47
- Cyclone Fani (2019): over 1.2 million people evacuated with days of advance warning, minimal loss of life
- India's cyclone prediction accuracy has improved from 100+ km error to ~50 km error, largely due to INSAT-3D/3DR data
- IMD (India Meteorological Department) relies heavily on ISRO satellite data

#### Other Scientific Satellites

**SARAS (Sub-millimetre Wave Astronomy Satellite)** [planned/concept stage]

**Chandrayaan, MOM, AstroSat, Aditya-L1, XPoSat** — covered in other topics

#### Summary of Applications
- **Weather forecasting**: Real-time cloud imagery, atmospheric profiles
- **Cyclone tracking**: Early warning saves thousands of lives
- **Monsoon monitoring**: Crucial for India's agriculture-dependent economy
- **Flood monitoring**: Using cloud imagery and rainfall estimation
- **Fog detection**: Northern India winter fog alerts for transport safety
- **Ocean monitoring**: Sea surface temperature, potential fishing zones, tsunami warning support
- **Climate studies**: Long-term weather patterns, climate change monitoring
- **Search and Rescue**: INSAT-3D/3DR carry transponders to detect distress signals from ships and aircraft

### Key Facts
- Kalpana-1 (2002) was India's first dedicated meteorological satellite, renamed after Kalpana Chawla
- INSAT-3D and INSAT-3DR are India's advanced weather satellites
- India's cyclone prediction has improved dramatically thanks to weather satellites
- Megha-Tropiques is an Indo-French joint mission studying tropical weather
- SARAL is an Indo-French ocean altimetry mission
- Satellite data has helped reduce cyclone casualties from thousands to dozens
- IMD uses ISRO satellite data for daily weather forecasts

### Important Terms
- **VHRR**: Very High Resolution Radiometer (weather imaging instrument)
- **IMD**: India Meteorological Department
- **Scatterometer**: Instrument measuring ocean surface winds
- **Altimeter**: Instrument measuring surface height (sea level)
- **CNES**: Centre National d'Études Spatiales (French space agency — partner for Megha-Tropiques and SARAL)
- **PFZ**: Potential Fishing Zone (derived from ocean satellite data)

### Common Confusions
| Confusion | Clarification |
|-----------|---------------|
| India has separate weather satellites from communication satellites | Many INSAT communication satellites ALSO carry weather payloads. Kalpana-1 was the first DEDICATED weather satellite |
| Kalpana-1 was named at launch | It was originally MetSat-1, renamed to Kalpana-1 in 2003 after Kalpana Chawla's death in the Columbia disaster |
| Weather monitoring only started with Kalpana-1 | INSAT-1B (1983) already carried weather instruments. Kalpana-1 (2002) was the first EXCLUSIVE weather satellite |
| Cyclone tracking requires radar satellites | Primary cyclone tracking uses visible/infrared weather satellite imagery from GEO. Radar provides supplementary data |
